Time Series Concepts

A time series is an ordered sequence of observations indexed by time: $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$. Unlike cross-sectional data, observations are temporally dependent — the value at time $t$ is correlated with past values $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, \ldots$

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define a time series and distinguish it from cross-sectional data
  • Identify and interpret the four components: trend, seasonality, cyclicality, and noise
  • Recognize additive vs. multiplicative series and select the appropriate model form

Core Definitions

Every time series can be characterized by four statistical moments:

Mean, Variance, Autocovariance, Autocorrelation
$$\mu_t = E[y_t]$$ $$\sigma_t^2 = \text{Var}(y_t) = E[(y_t - \mu_t)^2]$$ $$\gamma(t,s) = \text{Cov}(y_t, y_s) = E[(y_t-\mu_t)(y_s-\mu_s)]$$ $$\rho(t,s) = \frac{\gamma(t,s)}{\sqrt{\sigma_t^2 \sigma_s^2}}$$

Components of a Time Series

Most real-world series are composites of:

  • Trend ($T_t$) — long-run direction (upward GDP, downward unemployment)
  • Seasonality ($S_t$) — regular periodic patterns (monthly retail sales, quarterly earnings)
  • Cyclical ($C_t$) — irregular fluctuations tied to business cycles
  • Residual/Irregular ($\varepsilon_t$) — random noise after removing other components
Additive vs. Multiplicative Decomposition
$$y_t = T_t + S_t + C_t + \varepsilon_t \quad \text{(Additive)}$$ $$y_t = T_t \cdot S_t \cdot C_t \cdot \varepsilon_t \quad \text{(Multiplicative)}$$
💡
Use the additive model when seasonal variation is constant over time. Use the multiplicative model when seasonal swings grow proportionally with the level of the series.

Types of Time Series Processes

White Noise

The simplest process: $\varepsilon_t \sim WN(0, \sigma^2)$ — zero mean, constant variance, no autocorrelation.

White Noise Conditions
$$E[\varepsilon_t] = 0, \quad E[\varepsilon_t^2] = \sigma^2, \quad E[\varepsilon_t \varepsilon_s] = 0 \text{ for } t \neq s$$

Random Walk

A random walk accumulates shocks: $y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$. This process is non-stationary — its variance grows with time: $\text{Var}(y_t) = t\sigma^2$.

Stationarity & Unit Roots

Stationarity is the single most important concept in time series analysis. Most classical models (AR, ARIMA, VAR) assume some form of stationarity — violating it leads to spurious regressions and invalid inference.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish strict stationarity from weak (covariance) stationarity and state why the latter is sufficient in practice
  • Conduct and correctly interpret ADF and KPSS unit root tests
  • Transform a non-stationary series to stationarity using differencing or log transformation

Strict vs. Weak Stationarity

Strict (Strong) Stationarity
$$F(y_{t_1},\ldots,y_{t_k}) = F(y_{t_1+h},\ldots,y_{t_k+h}) \quad \forall\, h, k, t_1,\ldots,t_k$$

The entire joint distribution is time-invariant.

Weak (Covariance) Stationarity — most practical
$$E[y_t] = \mu \quad \text{(constant mean)}$$ $$\text{Var}(y_t) = \sigma^2 < \infty \quad \text{(constant variance)}$$ $$\text{Cov}(y_t, y_{t-k}) = \gamma(k) \quad \text{(depends only on lag } k \text{, not } t\text{)}$$
⚠️
Spurious Regression: Regressing two independent random walks on each other yields a high R² and significant t-statistics — purely due to non-stationarity. Always test for stationarity before modeling!

Unit Root Tests

Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) Test

The ADF test checks whether a unit root is present (i.e., the series is non-stationary). It estimates:

ADF Regression
$$\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \delta y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t$$

$H_0: \delta = 0$ (unit root — non-stationary) vs. $H_1: \delta < 0$ (stationary)
Reject $H_0$ when the ADF statistic is more negative than the critical value.

KPSS Test

The Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin (KPSS) test reverses the null: $H_0$ is stationarity. Using both ADF and KPSS provides cross-validation:

  • ADF: reject $H_0$ + KPSS: fail to reject $H_0$ → Stationary ✅
  • ADF: fail to reject $H_0$ + KPSS: reject $H_0$ → Non-stationary ❌

Making a Series Stationary

First Differencing (removes stochastic trend)
$$\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$$
Log Transform + Differencing (stabilizes variance)
$$\Delta \ln y_t = \ln y_t - \ln y_{t-1} \approx \frac{y_t - y_{t-1}}{y_{t-1}} \quad \text{(growth rate)}$$
Seasonal Differencing
$$\Delta_s y_t = y_t - y_{t-s}$$

Real-World Use Case

💱 Exchange Rate Forecasting

Central banks and FX traders test exchange rate series for unit roots. Most nominal exchange rates follow random walks (efficient market hypothesis), meaning short-term forecasting is near-impossible. However, real exchange rates (adjusted for inflation) often revert to purchasing power parity — a mean-reverting stationary process — enabling longer-horizon forecasting.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • A series is weakly stationary if its mean, variance, and autocovariance structure are constant over time — most classical models require at least this.

  • First-differencing eliminates linear trends and is the most common stationarity fix. Log-differencing ($\Delta \log y_t$) addresses both non-stationarity and proportional variance growth.
  • Non-stationarity without correction produces spurious regressions: artificially high R², significant t-tests, and meaningless forecasts.

ACF, PACF & Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) are diagnostic tools that reveal the memory structure of a time series and guide model order selection.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Compute and interpret ACF and PACF plots for an observed time series
  • Use ACF/PACF patterns to identify likely AR, MA, or ARMA model structures
  • Apply the Ljung-Box test to assess whether residual autocorrelation is statistically significant

Autocorrelation Function (ACF)

Sample ACF at Lag k
$$\hat{\rho}(k) = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t - \bar{y})(y_{t-k} - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t - \bar{y})^2}$$

The ACF measures the total correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$, including indirect correlations through intermediate lags.

Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)

The PACF isolates the direct correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$ after removing the influence of all intermediate lags $y_{t-1}, \ldots, y_{t-k+1}$.

PACF via Yule-Walker Equations
$$\hat{\phi}_{kk} = \text{Corr}(y_t - \hat{y}_t, \; y_{t-k} - \hat{y}_{t-k})$$ where $\hat{y}_t$ is the projection of $y_t$ onto $\{y_{t-1},\ldots,y_{t-k+1}\}$

Model Identification Rules

Model ACF Pattern PACF Pattern
AR(p) Decays exponentially / oscillates Cuts off sharply after lag p
MA(q) Cuts off sharply after lag q Decays exponentially
ARMA(p,q) Decays after lag q Decays after lag p
White Noise No significant spikes No significant spikes
✅
95% Confidence Bands: Under the null of no autocorrelation, the approximate 95% CI for the sample ACF is $\pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{T}}$. Spikes outside this band suggest significant autocorrelation at that lag.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • ACF measures total correlation at each lag including indirect paths; PACF removes those indirect paths — only the direct effect of lag $k$ remains.
  • Model identification rule: AR(p) → ACF decays gradually, PACF cuts off at lag $p$; MA(q) → PACF decays gradually, ACF cuts off at lag $q$; ARMA → both decay.

  • In practice, ACF/PACF identification is unreliable for mixed ARMA processes — information criteria (AIC, BIC) via auto.arima or pmdarima are more reliable for order selection.

Time Series Decomposition

Decomposition separates a time series into interpretable structural components, revealing the underlying patterns masked by noise.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Decompose a time series into trend, seasonal, and residual components using classical and STL methods
  • Choose between additive and multiplicative decomposition based on the series' variance structure
  • Assess residual randomness after decomposition to verify that structural patterns were fully captured

Classical Decomposition

Additive Decomposition Model
$$y_t = \hat{T}_t + \hat{S}_t + \hat{R}_t$$

Steps: (1) Estimate trend $\hat{T}_t$ via moving average; (2) Detrend: $y_t - \hat{T}_t$; (3) Estimate seasonality $\hat{S}_t$ by averaging over periods; (4) Residual $\hat{R}_t = y_t - \hat{T}_t - \hat{S}_t$

STL Decomposition

Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess (STL) is more robust than classical decomposition because it handles outliers and allows the seasonal component to change over time.

🎯
STL advantages: Works with any seasonal period, robust to outliers, seasonal component can evolve, handles missing values. Widely used in production forecasting. Note: Facebook Prophet uses a similar additive decomposition philosophy (trend + seasonality + holidays) but fits components via Bayesian curve-fitting rather than the STL iterative loess procedure.

X-11 / X-13ARIMA-SEATS

The official decomposition method used by the US Census Bureau and Eurostat for seasonal adjustment of GDP, employment, and CPI data. It extends classical decomposition with ARIMA modeling to handle irregular series and calendar effects (trading days, Easter, etc.).

AR & MA Models

The two building blocks of all classical linear time series models: the Autoregressive (AR) process and the Moving Average (MA) process.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write and interpret the AR(p) and MA(q) model equations using backshift operator notation
  • State the stationarity condition for AR models and the invertibility condition for MA models
  • Fit AR and MA models in Python and R and interpret the estimated parameters

Autoregressive Model — AR(p)

An AR(p) model expresses the current value as a linear function of its own past $p$ values plus a white-noise error.

AR(p) Definition
$$y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t$$ $$\Phi(L)\, y_t = c + \varepsilon_t \quad \text{where} \quad \Phi(L) = 1 - \phi_1 L - \cdots - \phi_p L^p$$

Stationarity requires all roots of $\Phi(z)=0$ to lie outside the unit circle.

💡
For AR(1): $y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$. Stationary when $|\phi| < 1$. When $\phi = 1$: random walk. When $\phi > 1$: explosive. Variance: $\sigma^2_y = \sigma^2_\varepsilon / (1-\phi^2)$

Moving Average Model — MA(q)

An MA(q) model expresses the current value as a linear combination of current and past error terms (shocks).

MA(q) Definition
$$y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2 \varepsilon_{t-2} + \cdots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}$$ $$y_t = \mu + \Theta(L)\, \varepsilon_t \quad \text{where} \quad \Theta(L) = 1 + \theta_1 L + \cdots + \theta_q L^q$$

MA processes are always stationary. Invertibility requires roots of $\Theta(z)=0$ outside the unit circle.

Real-World Use Case

📡 Interest Rate Modeling

The Vasicek model for short-term interest rates is essentially an AR(1) process in continuous time: $dr_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)dt + \sigma dW_t$. The discrete analog $r_t = (1-\kappa)\theta + \kappa r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ is estimated directly on Fed Funds rate data. Central banks use AR models as baseline forecasts for monetary policy decisions.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • AR(p) models current $y_t$ as a linear combination of $p$ past values plus noise; MA(q) models $y_t$ as a linear combination of $q$ past shocks plus noise.
  • AR stationarity requires all roots of the characteristic polynomial to lie outside the unit circle (equivalently, $|$AR coefficients$| < 1$ for AR(1)).
  • MA invertibility ensures a unique MA representation and is required for parameter estimation to be well-defined.
  • Both AR and MA are special cases of ARMA, which is itself a building block for the more general ARIMA and SARIMA frameworks covered in the next lessons.

ARMA & ARIMA Models

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Extend AR and MA to the full ARMA(p, q) framework and explain when combining both components is necessary
  • Apply $d$-order differencing to achieve stationarity and construct the ARIMA(p, d, q) model
  • Estimate ARIMA models via maximum likelihood and validate residuals using Ljung-Box and normality tests

ARMA(p, q)

The ARMA model combines both autoregressive and moving average components, providing a parsimonious representation of many real-world stationary processes.

ARMA(p, q) — Lag Polynomial Form
$$\Phi(L)\, y_t = \mu + \Theta(L)\, \varepsilon_t$$ $$(1 - \phi_1 L - \cdots - \phi_p L^p)\, y_t = \mu + (1 + \theta_1 L + \cdots + \theta_q L^q)\, \varepsilon_t$$

Stationarity: AR roots outside unit circle. Invertibility: MA roots outside unit circle.

ARIMA(p, d, q)

Most economic series are non-stationary. The ARIMA model handles this by applying $d$ rounds of differencing before fitting ARMA(p,q) to the stationary result.

ARIMA(p, d, q) — Integrated Model
$$\Phi(L)\, (1-L)^d\, y_t = \mu + \Theta(L)\, \varepsilon_t$$

$(1-L)^d$ applies differencing $d$ times. $d=1$ handles stochastic trend; $d=2$ handles quadratic trend.

📋
Box-Jenkins Methodology: (1) Identification — choose p, d, q via ACF/PACF; (2) Estimation — MLE or conditional least squares; (3) Diagnostic checking — residuals should be white noise; (4) Forecasting. If diagnostics fail, iterate.

Wold Decomposition Theorem

Wold's Theorem
$$y_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j} + \eta_t$$

Every covariance-stationary process has an infinite MA(∞) representation. ARMA models approximate this infinite representation parsimoniously with finite p and q.

SARIMA & ARIMAX

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Extend ARIMA to SARIMA by adding seasonal AR, differencing, and MA terms and interpret the $(P, D, Q)_s$ notation
  • Include exogenous variables in an ARIMAX model and understand when they improve forecast accuracy
  • Diagnose and correct residual seasonality remaining after ARIMA fitting

SARIMA — Seasonal ARIMA

When a series exhibits seasonal patterns (e.g., monthly sales, quarterly GDP), the SARIMA model adds seasonal AR, I, and MA terms to the ARIMA framework.

SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)ₛ
$$\Phi_p(L)\, \Phi_P(L^s)\, (1-L)^d\, (1-L^s)^D\, y_t = \Theta_q(L)\, \Theta_Q(L^s)\, \varepsilon_t$$

$s$ = seasonal period (12 for monthly, 4 for quarterly)
$\Phi_P(L^s) = 1 - \Phi_1 L^s - \cdots - \Phi_P L^{Ps}$ — seasonal AR
$(1-L^s)^D$ — seasonal differencing
$\Theta_Q(L^s)$ — seasonal MA

Notation Example

SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,1,1)₁₂ — often the best model for monthly data with trend and seasonality (Box-Jenkins identified this for AirPassengers). It applies both regular and seasonal differencing once each, with one AR and one MA term at both scales.

ARIMAX — ARIMA with Exogenous Variables

ARIMAX(p,d,q)
$$\Phi(L)\,(1-L)^d\, y_t = \beta' \mathbf{x}_t + \Theta(L)\,\varepsilon_t$$

$\mathbf{x}_t$ is a vector of exogenous predictors (e.g., advertising spend, temperature, interest rates). The model conditions on $\mathbf{x}_t$ — forecasting requires future values of $\mathbf{x}_t$ as inputs.

🎯
ARIMAX vs. regression with ARIMA errors: These are mathematically equivalent but ARIMAX imposes that $\mathbf{x}_t$ enters only through the mean, not the autocorrelation structure. The auto.arima function in R implements the latter (transfer function approach), which is generally more flexible.

Model Selection: AIC, BIC & ADF

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply AIC and BIC to compare competing ARIMA specifications and explain the difference in their penalty terms
  • Use auto.arima() / pmdarima.auto_arima() for automated, data-driven order selection
  • Conduct a full model diagnostic: residual ACF, Ljung-Box test, and normality check

Information Criteria

Information criteria trade off goodness of fit against model complexity, penalizing over-parameterization to prevent overfitting.

Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)
$$\text{AIC} = -2\ln\hat{L} + 2k$$

$\hat{L}$ = maximized log-likelihood, $k$ = number of parameters. Penalizes lightly — favors richer models.

Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC / SIC)
$$\text{BIC} = -2\ln\hat{L} + k\ln(T)$$

Replaces $2$ with $\ln(T)$. Since $\ln(T) > 2$ for $T > 7$, BIC penalizes complexity more heavily and is consistent (selects the true order as $T \to \infty$).

Hannan-Quinn Information Criterion (HQIC)
$$\text{HQIC} = -2\ln\hat{L} + 2k\ln\ln(T)$$

Lies between AIC and BIC. Also consistent but with lower penalty than BIC.

⚠️
Rule: Lower AIC/BIC = better model. Compare within the same dataset and same dependent variable. AIC tends to over-select order; BIC tends to under-select in small samples. In practice, cross-validation often outperforms both.

Augmented Dickey-Fuller — Full Treatment

ADF Regression (with trend)
$$\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \delta y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t$$

Three regression specifications: (1) no constant, (2) with constant, (3) with constant + trend. The lag length $p$ is chosen via AIC or BIC (Ng-Perron method).

Phillips-Perron (PP) Test

An alternative to ADF that uses a non-parametric correction for serial correlation, avoiding the lag selection issue. More powerful in some settings but size-distorted in small samples.

Vector Autoregression (VAR)

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Specify a VAR(p) model for a system of $K$ stationary variables and interpret each equation as a multivariate generalization of AR(p)
  • Determine the optimal lag order using information criteria (AIC, BIC, HQIC) and test VAR stability via eigenvalues
  • Forecast a multivariate system with a VAR model and quantify the curse of dimensionality ($K^2 p$ parameters)

VAR models extend the univariate AR framework to multiple time series, allowing each variable to depend on its own lags and the lags of all other variables in the system. This is the workhorse of empirical macroeconomics.

VAR(p) Definition

VAR(p) — Matrix Form
$$\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{c} + \mathbf{A}_1 \mathbf{y}_{t-1} + \mathbf{A}_2 \mathbf{y}_{t-2} + \cdots + \mathbf{A}_p \mathbf{y}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$$

$\mathbf{y}_t$ = $K \times 1$ vector of variables (e.g., GDP growth, inflation, interest rate)
$\mathbf{A}_i$ = $K \times K$ coefficient matrices at lag $i$
$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t \sim WN(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ — contemporaneously correlated innovations
Total parameters: $K^2 p + K$ (grows fast — curse of dimensionality)

Reduced Form vs. Structural VAR

The VAR above is the reduced form — no contemporaneous restrictions. A Structural VAR (SVAR) imposes economic restrictions to identify causal shocks:

SVAR — Identification via Cholesky Decomposition
$$\mathbf{B}_0 \mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{B}_1 \mathbf{y}_{t-1} + \cdots + \mathbf{B}_p \mathbf{y}_{t-p} + \mathbf{u}_t$$ $$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t = \mathbf{B}_0^{-1} \mathbf{u}_t, \quad \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{B}_0^{-1} \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{B}_0^{-1})'$$

Cholesky: order variables by causal priority (e.g., Fed rate → GDP → inflation). Lower-triangular $\mathbf{B}_0$ gives exact-identification.

Cointegration & VECM

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define cointegration and explain why it permits modeling non-stationary I(1) series in levels without spurious regression
  • Apply the Johansen trace and maximum eigenvalue tests to determine the cointegrating rank
  • Interpret the $\alpha$ (adjustment speed) and $\beta$ (cointegrating vectors) matrices in a VECM

Cointegration

Two (or more) individually I(1) series are cointegrated if there exists a linear combination that is I(0) — they share a long-run equilibrium relationship. This is the economic concept of long-run co-movement.

Cointegration Definition

If $y_{1t}, y_{2t} \sim I(1)$ but $y_{1t} - \beta y_{2t} \sim I(0)$, then they are cointegrated with cointegrating vector $(1, -\beta)$.

$$\mathbf{y}_t \sim CI(1,1) \iff \exists\, \boldsymbol{\beta}: \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{y}_t \sim I(0)$$
💎
Granger Representation Theorem: If $\mathbf{y}_t$ is cointegrated, it must have a Vector Error Correction (VECM) representation, and vice versa. This links long-run equilibrium (cointegration) with short-run dynamics (VECM).

Vector Error Correction Model (VECM)

VECM Representation
$$\Delta\mathbf{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\Pi}\,\mathbf{y}_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_i\Delta\mathbf{y}_{t-i} + \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$$ $$\boldsymbol{\Pi} = \boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}' \quad \text{where rank}(\boldsymbol{\Pi}) = r < K$$

$\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ = speed-of-adjustment matrix ($K \times r$)
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ = cointegrating vectors ($K \times r$)
$r$ = number of cointegrating relationships
$\boldsymbol{\Pi}\mathbf{y}_{t-1} = \boldsymbol{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{y}_{t-1})$ = error correction term

Johansen Test for Cointegration Rank

Johansen Trace and Max-Eigenvalue Statistics
$$\lambda_{\text{trace}}(r) = -T\sum_{i=r+1}^{K}\ln(1-\hat{\lambda}_i)$$ $$\lambda_{\text{max}}(r, r+1) = -T\ln(1-\hat{\lambda}_{r+1})$$

$H_0: \text{rank} \leq r$ vs. $H_1: \text{rank} > r$. Test sequentially starting from $r=0$. Stop when you fail to reject.

Impulse Response Functions (IRF)

IRFs trace the dynamic response of all variables in a VAR system to a one-time, one-unit shock in one variable's error term. They are the primary tool for identifying monetary policy transmission mechanisms.

Orthogonalized IRF (Cholesky)
$$\mathbf{y}_{t+h} = \sum_{i=0}^{h} \boldsymbol{\Phi}_i \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t+h-i}$$ $$\text{IRF}(h) = \frac{\partial \mathbf{y}_{t+h}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}_h \mathbf{P}$$

$\mathbf{P}$ = Cholesky factor of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ (lower triangular), giving orthogonal structural shocks. Bootstrap confidence bands via Hall percentile or asymptotic delta method.

Machine learning models like Random Forest don't assume linearity or Gaussian errors, making them attractive for complex, nonlinear time series. The key is proper feature engineering — transforming the raw series into tabular supervised learning format.

Feature Engineering for Time Series

Converting a time series into an ML-ready dataset involves creating lag features, rolling statistics, and calendar variables:

Supervised Learning Transformation
$$\underbrace{y_t}_{\text{target}} = f\!\left(\underbrace{y_{t-1},\ldots,y_{t-p}}_{\text{lag features}},\; \underbrace{\bar{y}_{t,w},\; \sigma_{t,w}}_{\text{rolling stats}},\; \underbrace{m_t, d_t, q_t}_{\text{calendar}},\; \underbrace{x_t}_{\text{exogenous}}\right) + \varepsilon_t$$

Common features include:

  • Lag features: $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, \ldots, y_{t-p}$ — direct history
  • Rolling mean/std: $\bar{y}_{t,7}$, $\bar{y}_{t,30}$ — local level and volatility
  • Calendar: month, day-of-week, quarter, holiday flag
  • Target-encoded seasonality: mean value per hour/month
  • First/seasonal difference: $\Delta y_t$, $\Delta_{12} y_t$ — stationarized signal

Random Forest Architecture

Ensemble Prediction
$$\hat{y}_t = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} T_b(\mathbf{x}_t)$$

$B$ = number of trees, $T_b$ = $b$-th decision tree trained on bootstrap sample with random feature subsets. Each split minimizes MSE (for regression). Out-of-bag (OOB) score estimates generalization without a hold-out set.

Gradient Boosting & XGBoost

Gradient boosting builds an ensemble sequentially — each new tree corrects the residual errors of the previous ensemble. XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) adds regularization, second-order gradients, and parallelism, making it the dominant algorithm in tabular ML competitions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the gradient boosting algorithm and derive the XGBoost objective function including its second-order Taylor expansion and regularization terms
  • Tune key hyperparameters (learning rate, max depth, subsample) and use early stopping with a held-out validation fold
  • Compare XGBoost with Random Forest and select the appropriate model based on the characteristics of the series

Gradient Boosting Framework

Sequential Ensemble Update
$$\hat{F}_m(\mathbf{x}) = \hat{F}_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta \cdot T_m(\mathbf{x})$$ $$T_m = \arg\min_{T} \sum_{i=1}^{n} L\!\left(y_i,\; \hat{F}_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) + T(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)$$

Each new tree $T_m$ fits negative gradient (pseudo-residuals): $r_i = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{F}}|_{\hat{F}=\hat{F}_{m-1}}$. $\eta$ = learning rate (shrinkage).

XGBoost Regularized Objective
$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[g_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i) + \frac{1}{2}h_i f_t^2(\mathbf{x}_i)\right] + \gamma T + \frac{1}{2}\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$$

$g_i = \partial_{\hat{y}}L$, $h_i = \partial^2_{\hat{y}}L$ (first/second-order gradients); $T$ = number of leaves; $\gamma,\lambda$ = regularization. Closed-form optimal leaf weights: $w^*_j = -G_j/(H_j+\lambda)$.

🏆
XGBoost hyperparameters for time series: Start with n_estimators=500, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=5, subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8. Use early stopping on a rolling validation fold. LightGBM and CatBoost are competitive alternatives with faster training.

LSTM Cell Equations

LSTM Gate Equations
$$\mathbf{f}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f) \quad \text{(forget gate)}$$ $$\mathbf{i}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i) \quad \text{(input gate)}$$ $$\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_c[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_c) \quad \text{(candidate cell)}$$ $$\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t \quad \text{(cell state update)}$$ $$\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o) \quad \text{(output gate)}$$ $$\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t) \quad \text{(hidden state)}$$

Facebook Prophet

Prophet Generative Model
$$y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + \varepsilon_t$$

$g(t)$ = trend (logistic growth or piecewise linear with automatic changepoint detection)
$s(t)$ = seasonality via Fourier series: $s(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[a_n\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi nt}{P}\right) + b_n\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi nt}{P}\right)\right]$

Logistic Growth Trend with Changepoints
$$g(t) = \frac{L(t)}{1 + \exp(-k(t)(t - m(t)))} + \sum_{j: t \geq s_j} \delta_j \cdot (t - s_j)$$

$k(t)$ = growth rate (adjusts at changepoints $s_j$), $m(t)$ = offset, $L(t)$ = carrying capacity, $\delta_j \sim \text{Laplace}(0, \tau)$ = changepoint magnitude.

State Space Models

State space models represent time series through a latent (unobserved) state vector that evolves over time, with observations being noisy functions of the state.

Linear Gaussian State Space (Kalman) Model
$$\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \mathbf{G}\mathbf{w}_t \quad \text{(state equation)}$$ $$\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_t \quad \text{(observation equation)}$$

$\mathbf{w}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q})$ process noise; $\mathbf{v}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{R})$ observation noise. All ARIMA and ETS models have state space representations!

🔗
Unifying Framework: ARIMA, Exponential Smoothing (ETS), structural time series models — all are special cases of the linear Gaussian state space model. This enables unified estimation via the Kalman filter and extensions (EKF, UKF, particle filters) for nonlinear/non-Gaussian cases.

The Kalman Filter

The Kalman filter is an optimal recursive Bayesian estimator for linear Gaussian state space models. It provides the minimum-variance unbiased estimate of the hidden state given all observations up to time $t$.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply the two-step Kalman filter (predict → update) and interpret each equation's role in fusing prior state estimates with new observations
  • Explain the Kalman gain and its behavior at the extremes (perfect model vs. perfect measurements)
  • Implement a tracking or smoothing application using the Kalman filter and interpret the resulting state estimates

Kalman Filter Algorithm

Prediction Step
$$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t-1} = \mathbf{F}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1|t-1}$$ $$\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{P}_{t-1|t-1}\mathbf{F}' + \mathbf{Q}$$
Update Step (after observing $\mathbf{y}_t$)
$$\mathbf{K}_t = \mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}'(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}' + \mathbf{R})^{-1} \quad \text{(Kalman gain)}$$ $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t-1} + \mathbf{K}_t(\mathbf{y}_t - \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t-1}) \quad \text{(state update)}$$ $$\mathbf{P}_{t|t} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}_t\mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1} \quad \text{(covariance update)}$$

Innovation $\boldsymbol{\nu}_t = \mathbf{y}_t - \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t|t-1}$ measures surprise. When $\mathbf{K}_t \to \mathbf{0}$: ignore observation (trust model). When $\mathbf{K}_t \to \mathbf{H}^{-1}$: ignore model (trust observation).

Combining multiple forecasting models almost always outperforms any single model — a robust empirical finding known as the "forecast combination puzzle" (Timmermann, 2006; Stock & Watson, 2004).

🎯 Learning Objectives

Simple Combination Methods

Equal-Weight Combination
$$\hat{y}_t^{(c)} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{y}_t^{(m)}$$
Optimal Weight Combination (Bates-Granger, 1969)
$$\hat{y}_t^{(c)} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m \hat{y}_t^{(m)}, \quad \mathbf{w}^* = \frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}$$

$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ = forecast error covariance matrix estimated from holdout. When errors are correlated, optimal weights can be negative ("shrinkage toward equal weights" improves out-of-sample performance).

Stacking (Meta-Learning)

Train a second-level model on the outputs of base models. The meta-learner learns to weight models optimally given the input context.

Stacking Framework
$$\hat{y}_t = f_{\text{meta}}\!\left(\hat{y}_t^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{y}_t^{(M)}, \mathbf{z}_t\right)$$

$\mathbf{z}_t$ = additional context features. $f_{\text{meta}}$ can be linear regression, ridge, or gradient boosting. Must use out-of-fold predictions to avoid leakage.

Point Forecast Metrics

MAE, RMSE, MAPE, sMAPE
$$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}|y_{T+h} - \hat{y}_{T+h}|$$ $$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}(y_{T+h} - \hat{y}_{T+h})^2}$$ $$\text{MAPE} = \frac{100}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left|\frac{y_{T+h}-\hat{y}_{T+h}}{y_{T+h}}\right|$$ $$\text{MASE} = \frac{\text{MAE}}{\frac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}|y_t - y_{t-m}|}$$

MASE (Mean Absolute Scaled Error) scales by the naive seasonal forecast error, making it scale-free and meaningful even when $y_t \approx 0$. Preferred metric in the M4 and M5 competitions.

Diebold-Mariano Test

Formally tests whether two forecast models have equal predictive accuracy:

Diebold-Mariano (DM) Test Statistic
$$d_t = L(e_{1t}) - L(e_{2t}), \quad \bar{d} = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{t=1}^{H}d_t$$ $$\text{DM} = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\hat{V}(\bar{d})/H}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

$e_{it} = y_t - \hat{y}_{it}$ = forecast errors; $L(\cdot)$ = loss function (MSE, MAE, etc.); $\hat{V}(\bar{d})$ = HAC variance estimator (Newey-West) to account for serial correlation in loss differentials. $H_0$: equal predictive accuracy.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • MAPE fails when $y_t \approx 0$ (division by zero) and is asymmetric (larger penalties for over-forecasting). Prefer MASE for series that pass through or near zero; prefer RMSE when large errors are especially costly.
  • MASE scales by the in-sample naive forecast error and requires specifying the correct seasonal period $m$ — using $m=1$ for a monthly seasonal series dramatically inflates the MASE value.
  • The Diebold-Mariano (DM) test uses a Newey-West HAC variance estimator to account for autocorrelated loss differentials — this is critical for multi-step-ahead forecasts where errors are serially correlated.
  • A 95% prediction interval should contain the actual value 95% of the time — calibration. Always back-test your prediction intervals; they are routinely too narrow in practice due to model misspecification.

Module 6 · Lesson 1

Data Preparation for Forecasting

Real-world time series data is messy. Proper preparation is the foundation of reliable forecasts — garbage in, garbage out applies with particular force in temporal modeling.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Handle missing values in time series using imputation methods appropriate to the missingness mechanism (LOCF, interpolation, model-based)
  • Detect and treat outliers without introducing look-ahead bias
  • Scale and transform features correctly to prevent data leakage between train and test sets

Missing Value Treatment

⚠️
Never use forward-looking imputation! If you fill missing values with a method that uses future data (e.g., linear interpolation using points after the gap), you introduce look-ahead bias. Use only backward-looking methods in production pipelines.
  • Last observation carried forward (LOCF) — simple, causal, good for low-frequency series
  • Seasonal interpolation — fill gap with same period from prior year
  • Kalman smoothing — optimal under state space model assumptions
  • Multiple imputation — for exploratory analysis only (not production)

Outlier Detection & Treatment

IQR-Based Detection
$$\text{Outlier if } y_t < Q_1 - 1.5\cdot\text{IQR} \text{ or } y_t > Q_3 + 1.5\cdot\text{IQR}$$
STL-Based Residual Detection
$$y_t \text{ is outlier if } |\hat{R}_t| > k \cdot \hat{\sigma}_R, \quad k \in [3,5]$$

Decompose with STL, flag residuals beyond $k$ robust standard deviations. Replace outliers with $\hat{T}_t + \hat{S}_t$ (the deterministic component).

Scaling & Transformations

Box-Cox Transformation
$$y^{(\lambda)}_t = \begin{cases} \frac{y_t^\lambda - 1}{\lambda} & \lambda \neq 0 \\ \ln(y_t) & \lambda = 0 \end{cases}$$

Stabilizes variance. $\lambda=0$ (log), $\lambda=0.5$ (square root), $\lambda=1$ (no transform). Choose $\lambda$ via Guerrero method or profile log-likelihood.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • Forward-fill (LOCF) is the only imputation method safe to use in a production pipeline — it uses only past information. Backward-fill and mean imputation introduce look-ahead bias and must never be applied before splitting train/test sets.
  • Outlier detection and treatment must be performed on the training set only; outlier thresholds must not be influenced by test-set values.
  • Scaling (StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler) must be fit on training data only and then applied to both train and test — fitting on the full dataset leaks test-set statistics into the model.
  • Box-Cox transformation stabilizes variance and can improve model performance for series with heteroskedastic noise; the $\lambda$ parameter should be selected on training data and applied consistently through the pipeline.

Time Series Cross-Validation & Backtesting

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Implement expanding and rolling window walk-forward validation and choose between them based on the presence of structural breaks
  • Correctly report averaged metrics across CV folds and understand why walk-forward estimates are often pessimistic
  • Design a model selection process that uses CV estimates for tuning and reserves a hold-out test set for final evaluation

Walk-Forward Validation

The gold standard for time series model evaluation. At each fold, the model is trained on all data up to time $t$ and evaluated on a fixed window ahead — simulating real deployment.

Expanding Window (Walk-Forward)
$$\text{Fold } k: \quad \text{Train} = \{y_1,\ldots,y_{t_k}\}, \quad \text{Test} = \{y_{t_k+1},\ldots,y_{t_k+h}\}$$ $$t_k = t_{\text{init}} + (k-1)\cdot\text{step}, \quad k = 1,\ldots,K$$
Rolling Window (Fixed-Size Train)
$$\text{Fold } k: \quad \text{Train} = \{y_{t_k-W+1},\ldots,y_{t_k}\}, \quad \text{Test} = \{y_{t_k+1},\ldots,y_{t_k+h}\}$$

Use rolling window when you suspect parameter instability (structural breaks). Use expanding window when more data consistently helps.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • Walk-forward (expanding window) CV is preferred when the full history is informative; rolling window CV is preferred when older data may no longer be relevant due to structural breaks or distribution shift.
  • CV estimates are pessimistic (lower than true out-of-sample performance) because the earliest folds train on less data than the final deployed model — this is acceptable for model comparison but not for absolute accuracy reporting.
  • Never use the test set for model selection or tuning. Use walk-forward CV for all model comparison decisions; reserve the test set for a single final evaluation after all decisions are made.
  • Report both the mean and standard deviation of CV fold metrics — high variance across folds indicates an unstable model that may not generalize reliably.

Production Forecasting Pipelines

A production-grade forecasting system requires automation, monitoring, retraining triggers, and robust uncertainty quantification — not just a trained model.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Design a production forecasting pipeline with stages for data ingestion, preprocessing, training, evaluation, and serving
  • Implement data drift detection and forecast monitoring to trigger automated retraining
  • Apply champion/challenger model evaluation to safely deploy improved models without disrupting production

Pipeline Architecture

🏗️
Typical production pipeline stages:
1. Ingestion — pull data from source (DB, API, warehouse)
2. Validation — schema checks, freshness, outlier flags
3. Preprocessing — imputation, outlier treatment, transformations
4. Feature Engineering — lags, rolling stats, calendar features
5. Modeling — fit one or more models from registry
6. Evaluation — walk-forward CV, choose champion
7. Inference — generate forecasts with confidence intervals
8. Serving — write to DB/API, trigger alerts
9. Monitoring — track forecast error, MAPE drift, trigger retraining

Prediction Intervals

Bootstrap Prediction Intervals
$$\hat{y}_{T+h} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \hat{\sigma}_h$$ $$\hat{\sigma}_h^2 = \hat{\sigma}_\varepsilon^2 \cdot \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2 \quad \text{(analytical, ARIMA)}$$

For ML models: use conformal prediction or quantile regression to obtain calibrated intervals without distributional assumptions.

Conformal Prediction Interval
$$\text{PI}_{1-\alpha}(x_{T+1}) = \left[\hat{y}_{T+1} - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}, \; \hat{y}_{T+1} + \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}\right]$$

$\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}$ = $(1-\alpha/2)$-quantile of calibration residuals $\{|y_t - \hat{y}_t|\}$. Marginally valid: covers $100(1-\alpha)\%$ of future points without Gaussian assumptions.

Real-World Use Case

🏪 Retail Inventory Optimization

A large retailer runs forecasting pipelines for 100,000+ SKU-location combinations daily. The system: ingests POS scan data → validates for anomalies (store closures, data feed failures) → runs ensemble of ETS + XGBoost + store-level RF → generates 4–8 week demand forecasts → feeds safety stock calculations → triggers replenishment orders. Conformal prediction intervals drive the safety stock formula, targeting 98.5% in-stock rate while minimizing excess inventory. A 1% reduction in forecast MAPE across this portfolio yields ~$50M in inventory cost savings annually.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • A production pipeline is not just model training — it encompasses data validation, feature computation, prediction serving, monitoring, and automated retraining triggers.
  • Model drift monitoring should track two dimensions: data drift (input feature distributions shift) and forecast drift (errors systematically increase). Both require separate alarms and response protocols.
  • Champion/challenger deployment lets you safely test a new model on a small traffic fraction before full promotion, comparing live performance rather than relying only on historical backtests.
  • Full-pipeline integration tests — retraining a model end-to-end from raw data to evaluated forecast — are essential for catching silent failures (e.g., stale feature stores, schema changes, silent numeric overflows) that unit tests miss.
