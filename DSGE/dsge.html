What is a DSGE Model?

A DSGE model is a macroeconomic model that describes the entire economy as a system of optimizing agents — households, firms, and policymakers — whose decisions interact through markets that always clear. The Dynamic dimension means agents optimize over an infinite horizon, trading off present against future. The Stochastic dimension means random shocks — technology surprises, monetary policy errors, preference shifts — drive business cycle fluctuations. General Equilibrium means we track every market simultaneously rather than studying one sector in isolation.

At their core, DSGE models are microfounded: every behavioral equation is derived explicitly from an optimization problem with well-defined preferences and constraints. This stands in sharp contrast to the ad hoc structural models (like large-scale macroeconometric models) that dominated before the 1980s, whose parameters were vulnerable to the Lucas Critique.

💡
The Lucas Critique (1976)
Robert Lucas showed that the parameters of reduced-form econometric models are not structural — they shift whenever policy changes, because agents' optimal decisions depend on the policy regime. DSGE models sidestep this by grounding behavior in deep, invariant parameters (preferences, technology) that do not change with policy.

A Brief History

1954–1972
Arrow-Debreu General Equilibrium
Walrasian foundations established: competitive equilibria exist and are Pareto optimal. Provides the micro underpinnings for later macroeconomic models.
1976
Lucas Critique
Robert Lucas argues policy analysis using structural macroeconometric models is flawed. Paves the way for microfounded models with rational expectations.
1982
Kydland & Prescott — Real Business Cycle
The founding paper of modern DSGE. Shows that technology shocks alone, propagated through a neoclassical growth model with optimizing agents, can explain a significant fraction of business cycle variance. Nobel Prize 2004.
1983 / 1993
Calvo Pricing & Taylor Rule
Guillermo Calvo introduces staggered price-setting (a random fraction of firms cannot adjust each period). John Taylor proposes a simple interest-rate rule linking the policy rate to inflation and output — the building blocks of the New Keynesian model.
1999
New Keynesian Synthesis
Woodford, Clarida-Galí-Gertler, and Galí-Monacelli formalize the three-equation New Keynesian (NK) model: IS curve, Phillips curve, Taylor rule. This becomes the standard workhorse for monetary policy analysis.
2003–2007
Smets & Wouters — Medium-Scale DSGE
Smets and Wouters estimate a large-scale NK DSGE model on Euro Area and US data using Bayesian methods. The model matches the data comparably to a Bayesian VAR — cementing DSGE as a tool for empirical central banking.
2010–present
HANK, Financial Frictions, Open Economy
Post-GFC research incorporates financial intermediation (BGG accelerator, Gertler-Kiyotaki), heterogeneous agents (HANK by Kaplan-Moll-Violante), and open-economy dynamics. The frontier now lives at the intersection of DSGE and structural heterogeneous-agent models.

Why DSGE Models Matter

🏛️ Policy Analysis

Central banks worldwide — the Fed, ECB, Bank of England — use DSGE models to analyze the effects of interest rate changes, forward guidance, and unconventional monetary policy. The models provide counterfactual experiments that real-world data cannot.

🔬 Structural Identification

By imposing theory-derived restrictions, DSGE models achieve structural identification — we can separate demand shocks from supply shocks, monetary from fiscal impulses — something reduced-form VARs struggle with.

📈 Forecasting

Bayesian DSGE models estimated on historical data produce competitive short-run forecasts and — crucially — conditional forecasts under different policy scenarios that purely statistical models cannot provide.

🎓 Theoretical Coherence

Every equation is derived from a first-order condition. There are no free parameters added for fit — each coefficient has a precise economic interpretation. This discipline keeps models interpretable and falsifiable.

⚠️
Criticisms & Limitations
DSGE models have faced criticism: they assumed away financial frictions before the 2008 crisis (Stiglitz, Romer), rely on the representative-agent assumption that misses distributional effects, and require strong parametric assumptions on preferences and technology. Modern DSGE research actively addresses these limitations through HANK models and financial-friction extensions.

Households

Derive household optimality conditions — the Euler equation, labor supply, and budget constraint — from first principles.

The representative household lives forever, maximizes expected lifetime utility over consumption and labor, and faces a nominal budget constraint. Every behavioral equation we use — from the IS curve to labor market conditions — follows from this optimization problem.

Preferences

The household's lifetime utility is:

Lifetime Utility
$$\mathcal{U}_0 = E_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t \, U(C_t, N_t) = E_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t \left[\frac{C_t^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma} - \frac{N_t^{1+\varphi}}{1+\varphi}\right]$$

where $\beta \in (0,1)$ is the discount factor, $C_t$ is consumption, $N_t$ is hours worked, $\sigma > 0$ is the inverse of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution (IES), and $\varphi > 0$ is the inverse of the Frisch elasticity of labor supply.

📌
Discount factor β
Calibrated to $\beta \approx 0.99$ at quarterly frequency, implying an annual real interest rate of about 4% in steady state: $\bar{R} = 1/\beta \approx 1.04$.
📌
IES = 1/σ
When $\sigma = 1$ utility is logarithmic. The Frisch elasticity $1/\varphi$ governs how much labor responds to wage changes holding marginal utility of wealth constant.

Budget Constraint

The household earns labor income, holds one-period nominal bonds $B_t$, receives firm profits $\Pi_t$, and pays lump-sum taxes $T_t$:

Nominal Budget Constraint
$$P_t C_t + Q_t B_t \leq B_{t-1} + W_t N_t + \Pi_t - T_t$$

where $P_t$ is the price level, $Q_t = 1/(1+i_t)$ is the price of a nominal bond (inverse of the gross nominal interest rate $1+i_t$), and $W_t$ is the nominal wage.

First-Order Conditions

Setting up the Lagrangian and differentiating with respect to $C_t$, $N_t$, and $B_t$ yields three optimality conditions:

Euler Equation (Consumption)

Equates the marginal cost of saving (foregone consumption today) with the expected marginal benefit (consumption tomorrow):

Consumption Euler Equation
$$C_t^{-\sigma} = \beta \, (1+i_t) \, E_t\!\left[\frac{C_{t+1}^{-\sigma}}{1+\pi_{t+1}}\right] = \beta \, (1+r_t) \, E_t\!\left[C_{t+1}^{-\sigma}\right]$$

where $1 + r_t = (1+i_t)/(1+\pi_{t+1})$ is the gross real interest rate and $\pi_{t+1} = P_{t+1}/P_t - 1$ is the inflation rate. This is the most important equation in the model: it says consumption grows when the real interest rate exceeds the discount rate.

Labor Supply

Intratemporal Labor Supply Condition
$$\frac{W_t}{P_t} = C_t^{\sigma} \, N_t^{\varphi}$$

The real wage equals the household's marginal rate of substitution between leisure and consumption: the disutility of working an extra hour must equal the real purchasing power of the marginal wage.

Transversality Condition

$$\lim_{T\to\infty} \beta^T \, E_t\!\left[\lambda_{t+T} B_{t+T}\right] = 0$$

Rules out Ponzi schemes: households cannot borrow indefinitely. Together with the budget constraint and Euler equation, this pins down a unique optimal consumption path.

Log-Linearized Euler Equation

Log-linearizing around the deterministic steady state (denoted with bars, deviations with lower case) gives the IS equation we use throughout the course:

Log-Linearized Euler (IS Equation)
$$c_t = E_t[c_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma}(i_t - E_t[\pi_{t+1}] - \rho)$$

where $\rho = -\ln\beta$ is the natural rate of interest in steady state, and lowercase letters denote log-deviations from steady state. This says: when the real interest rate exceeds its natural level, consumption falls — the standard negative IS relationship.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Firms & Price Setting

Understand how monopolistically competitive firms produce differentiated goods, set prices under Calvo staggering, and give rise to the New Keynesian Phillips Curve.

The production side of a New Keynesian DSGE model features two layers: a perfectly competitive final goods firm that aggregates differentiated varieties into a single consumption good, and a continuum of intermediate goods producers operating under monopolistic competition with staggered price-setting.

Final Goods Aggregator (Dixit-Stiglitz)

A representative final-goods firm aggregates a continuum of differentiated varieties $Y_t(j)$, $j \in [0,1]$, using the constant-elasticity-of-substitution (CES) technology:

CES Aggregator
$$Y_t = \left(\int_0^1 Y_t(j)^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}} dj\right)^{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}$$

where $\varepsilon > 1$ is the elasticity of substitution between varieties. Cost minimization yields the demand curve for each variety:

Demand for Variety j
$$Y_t(j) = \left(\frac{P_t(j)}{P_t}\right)^{-\varepsilon} Y_t, \qquad P_t = \left(\int_0^1 P_t(j)^{1-\varepsilon} dj\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}$$

The price elasticity of demand is $\varepsilon$, and the implied markup in steady state is $\mu = \varepsilon/(\varepsilon-1)$. Typical calibration: $\varepsilon = 6$ giving a 20% markup.

Intermediate Goods Producers

Each firm $j$ uses only labor (for simplicity) with production function:

$$Y_t(j) = A_t \, N_t(j)^{1-\alpha}$$

where $A_t$ is aggregate technology (common to all firms) and $\alpha \in [0,1)$ controls diminishing returns to labor. With constant returns ($\alpha = 0$): $Y_t(j) = A_t N_t(j)$. The nominal marginal cost is:

Nominal Marginal Cost
$$MC_t = \frac{W_t}{A_t (1-\alpha) N_t(j)^{-\alpha}} = \frac{W_t}{A_t}$$

(constant returns case, α = 0)

Real marginal cost $mc_t = MC_t / P_t = W_t/(P_t A_t)$ is the key driver of inflation in the Phillips curve.

Calvo Pricing

The central nominal friction in New Keynesian models is Calvo (1983) staggered price-setting. In each period:

  • With probability $\theta \in (0,1)$, a firm cannot reset its price (it's stuck at the old price).
  • With probability $(1-\theta)$, a firm can freely choose a new optimal price $P_t^*$.

This means the average duration of a price is $1/(1-\theta)$ periods. At $\theta = 0.75$ (a common calibration), firms reset prices every 4 quarters on average.

🔬
Why Calvo and not Menu Costs?
Calvo pricing is chosen for tractability: the fraction of price-setters is exogenous, making the model linear. Menu-cost models (Golosov-Lucas) give discrete price changes like in the data but are harder to solve. Calvo remains the workhorse assumption in most central bank DSGE models.

Optimal Reset Price

A firm resetting its price at $t$ chooses $P_t^*$ to maximize the expected present value of profits over the (random) interval before the next reset:

Optimal Price-Setting Problem
$$\max_{P_t^*} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (\beta\theta)^k E_t\!\left[\Lambda_{t,t+k} \left(P_t^* - MC_{t+k}\right) Y_{t+k}(j)\right]$$

The FOC yields the optimal reset price as a markup over a weighted average of future marginal costs:

Optimal Reset Price
$$P_t^* = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon - 1} \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\beta\theta)^k E_t[MC_{t+k} Y_{t+k}]}{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\beta\theta)^k E_t[Y_{t+k}]}$$

The weight $\beta\theta$ reflects discounting ($\beta$) and the probability of being stuck at this price for another period ($\theta$). Future marginal costs matter because the price may still be in effect then.

Price Level Evolution

The aggregate price level evolves according to:

$$P_t^{1-\varepsilon} = (1-\theta)(P_t^*)^{1-\varepsilon} + \theta P_{t-1}^{1-\varepsilon}$$

A fraction $(1-\theta)$ of firms set the new price $P_t^*$; the rest keep $P_{t-1}$ (their old price from last period).

The New Keynesian Phillips Curve

Log-linearizing the optimal price-setting condition and price level evolution around zero inflation yields the celebrated New Keynesian Phillips Curve (NKPC):

New Keynesian Phillips Curve
$$\pi_t = \beta \, E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa \, \hat{mc}_t$$

where $\hat{mc}_t$ is log-deviation of real marginal cost from steady state, and:

Slope of the NKPC
$$\kappa = \frac{(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)}{\theta}$$

Note that $\kappa$ is decreasing in $\theta$: more price stickiness flattens the Phillips curve — firms don't pass cost increases into prices as quickly. As $\theta \to 0$ (flexible prices), $\kappa \to \infty$ and inflation is purely forward-looking. Substituting the labor market equilibrium, $\hat{mc}_t = (\sigma + \varphi) x_t / (1 + \varphi\varepsilon)$, and we get the output-gap formulation:

NKPC in Output Gap Form
$$\pi_t = \beta \, E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa_x \, x_t, \qquad \kappa_x = \frac{(1-\theta)(1-\beta\theta)}{\theta} \cdot \frac{\sigma + \varphi}{1+\varphi\varepsilon}$$

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

beta    = 0.99   # Discount factor
theta   = 0.75   # Calvo parameter (75% of firms cannot reset each quarter)
epsilon = 6.0    # Elasticity of substitution (20% markup)
sigma   = 1.0    # Inverse IES

Monetary & Fiscal Policy

Characterize monetary policy rules, government budget constraints, and how policy affects equilibrium dynamics.

The Taylor Rule

Rather than solving for the optimal policy under commitment, most DSGE models use a simple Taylor-type interest rate rule. John Taylor (1993) proposed that the Fed's policy rate could be well described by:

Taylor Rule (1993 Original)
$$i_t = \bar{r} + \pi^* + \phi_\pi(\pi_t - \pi^*) + \phi_y \, x_t$$

where $\bar{r}$ is the equilibrium real rate, $\pi^*$ is the inflation target, $\phi_\pi > 0$ is the policy response to inflation, and $\phi_y \geq 0$ is the response to the output gap.

⚠️
The Taylor Principle
Determinacy of equilibrium requires $\phi_\pi > 1$ — the central bank must raise the real interest rate when inflation rises, not just the nominal rate. If $\phi_\pi \leq 1$, inflation becomes self-fulfilling: a belief in higher future inflation triggers higher actual inflation, which the CB accommodates, validating the belief. This is "indeterminacy."

Generalized Taylor Rule

In estimated models (e.g., Smets-Wouters), the rule includes interest rate smoothing:

Taylor Rule with Smoothing
$$i_t = \rho_i \, i_{t-1} + (1-\rho_i)\left[\bar{r} + \pi^* + \phi_\pi(\pi_t - \pi^*) + \phi_y \, x_t\right] + \phi_{\Delta y}(x_t - x_{t-1}) + \varepsilon_t^{MP}$$

where $\rho_i \in (0,1)$ is the interest rate smoothing parameter and $\phi_{\Delta y}$ is the response to output growth. Typical estimated values: $\rho_i \approx 0.8$, $\phi_\pi \approx 1.7$, $\phi_y \approx 0.1$.

📐 Standard Calibration

Taylor's original (1993) values: $\phi_\pi = 1.5$, $\phi_y = 0.5$. These have become the benchmark "Taylor rule" coefficients against which estimated rules are compared. Note: Taylor used the GDP gap with coefficient 0.5; many models use 0.125 in quarterly models (0.5/4).

🔐 Policy Credibility

A higher $\phi_\pi$ is associated with better anchoring of inflation expectations. During the Volcker disinflation (1979–83), the Fed adopted a very high implicit $\phi_\pi$, sharply raising real rates to break inflation expectations — at the cost of a deep recession.

Market Clearing

In a closed-economy model with government spending $G_t$, goods market clearing requires:

Goods Market Clearing
$$Y_t = C_t + G_t + I_t$$

In the basic three-equation model without investment or government (or treating $G_t$ as a demand shock absorbed into $r_t^n$), market clearing simplifies to $Y_t = C_t$, so the output gap $x_t \equiv \hat{y}_t - \hat{y}_t^n$ equals the consumption gap.

Government Budget Constraint & Fiscal Policy

Government Flow Budget Constraint
$$P_t G_t + B_{t-1} = P_t T_t + Q_t B_t$$

Under Ricardian equivalence (Barro 1974), the timing of taxes vs. bond issuance is irrelevant: households internalize the government's budget constraint and save the full amount of any tax cut to pay future taxes. DSGE models often assume Ricardian equivalence to simplify. Non-Ricardian households (hand-to-mouth consumers) introduce fiscal multipliers above unity — a key HANK insight.

📊
Fiscal Multipliers in DSGE
In a standard DSGE model with Ricardian equivalence and flexible prices, the government spending multiplier is less than 1 (crowding out). With sticky prices and a binding ZLB, multipliers can exceed 1 (Christiano-Eichenbaum-Rebelo 2011). The multiplier depends critically on the monetary policy response and the proportion of hand-to-mouth consumers.

Key Equations & Log-Linearization

Master the three-equation New Keynesian model and the log-linearization technique that transforms nonlinear equilibrium conditions into a tractable linear system.

The Three-Equation New Keynesian Model

After log-linearizing around the zero-inflation steady state and combining households' Euler equation, firms' price-setting, and the Taylor rule, we arrive at the canonical three-equation system:

IS Curve (Dynamic)
$$x_t = E_t[x_{t+1}] - \frac{1}{\sigma}\left(i_t - E_t[\pi_{t+1}] - r_t^n\right)$$
New Keynesian Phillips Curve
$$\pi_t = \beta \, E_t[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa \, x_t + u_t$$
Monetary Policy Rule (Taylor)
$$i_t = \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t + v_t$$

where $x_t = y_t - y_t^n$ is the output gap (log deviation of actual output from flexible-price potential), $r_t^n$ is the natural rate of interest (the real rate that would prevail with flexible prices), $u_t$ is a cost-push shock, and $v_t$ is a monetary policy shock.

📌 Reading the IS Curve

The output gap today equals the expected future gap minus the real interest rate gap $(i_t - E_t\pi_{t+1} - r_t^n)$. When the central bank sets a real rate above $r_t^n$, it contracts demand — the gap falls. This captures the standard IS relationship in microfounded form.

📌 Reading the NKPC

Inflation today is the discounted sum of all future output gaps. Iterating forward: $\pi_t = \kappa \sum_{k=0}^\infty \beta^k E_t[x_{t+k}]$. Inflation is purely forward-looking — it reflects expected future economic activity, not past inflation, unlike backward-looking Phillips curves.

Extending to Capital and Investment

The three-equation model is a deliberately stripped-down benchmark. Real central-bank DSGE models (e.g., Smets-Wouters 2003/2007) add capital accumulation and investment adjustment costs, which are essential for matching the persistence of output and the volatility of investment.

Capital Accumulation
$$K_{t+1} = (1-\delta)\,K_t + I_t\!\left[1 - S\!\left(\frac{I_t}{I_{t-1}}\right)\right]$$

where $\delta$ is the depreciation rate, $I_t$ is gross investment, and $S(\cdot)$ is an investment adjustment cost function with $S(1)=0$, $S'(1)=0$, $S''(1) = \kappa_I > 0$ (Christiano-Eichenbaum-Evans 2005). When $\kappa_I = 0$, costs vanish and we recover the standard frictionless case.

Tobin's Q and Investment
$$Q_t = E_t\!\left[\beta \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\left((1-\delta)Q_{t+1} + r_{t+1}^k\right)\right]$$ $$\hat{\imath}_t = \frac{1}{\kappa_I} \hat{q}_t$$

The log-linearized investment equation links investment to Tobin's Q ($\hat{q}_t$, the shadow price of capital): investment rises when the value of an additional unit of installed capital exceeds its replacement cost. The coefficient $1/\kappa_I$ falls when adjustment costs are large, flattening the investment response.

📌
The Extended 5-Equation System
Adding capital and investment to the three-equation model gives: (1) IS curve (with investment), (2) NKPC, (3) Taylor rule, (4) capital accumulation, (5) investment/Tobin's Q equation. Habit formation in consumption (parameter $h$) and wage stickiness (a wage Phillips curve) bring the system to the 7-equation Smets-Wouters scale used by central banks.
⚠️
Why Capital Matters for Estimation
Without capital, the model has no endogenous persistence beyond shock autocorrelation — implying implausibly fast reversion of output. Adding capital (and adjustment costs) gives the model the "hump-shaped" IRF for output that matches the data. The Smets-Wouters model has 10 structural equations and 7 structural shocks — far beyond the toy three-equation system.

Log-Linearization Technique

Log-linearization approximates nonlinear equations around a steady state. The key steps are:

🔧 Step-by-Step Log-Linearization

Step 1: Write the nonlinear equation, e.g., the Euler equation $C_t^{-\sigma} = \beta R_t C_{t+1}^{-\sigma}$.

Step 2: Divide through by the steady-state version: $\bar{C}^{-\sigma} = \beta \bar{R} \bar{C}^{-\sigma}$, confirming $\beta \bar{R} = 1$.

Step 3: Write $C_t = \bar{C}(1 + \hat{c}_t)$, $R_t = \bar{R}(1+\hat{r}_t)$ where hats denote proportional deviations from steady state.

Step 4: Substitute and use the first-order approximation $(1+x)^n \approx 1 + nx$ for small $x$.

Step 5: Cancel steady-state terms, drop products of deviations (second-order small).

Result: $-\sigma \hat{c}_t = \hat{r}_t - \sigma \hat{c}_{t+1}$ → $\hat{c}_t = \hat{c}_{t+1} - \hat{r}_t/\sigma$.

General Log-Linearization Formula
$$\text{If } Z_t = X_t^\alpha Y_t^\beta, \text{ then } \hat{z}_t \approx \alpha \hat{x}_t + \beta \hat{y}_t$$

Products become sums, ratios become differences, and constants drop out. This powerful technique reduces complex nonlinear systems to linear difference equations solvable with standard matrix methods.

Natural Rate of Interest

The natural rate $r_t^n$ is the real interest rate consistent with flexible prices (gap = 0, inflation = 0). With technology shocks $a_t$ (AR(1) with persistence $\rho_a$):

$$r_t^n = \rho - \sigma \frac{(1-\rho_a)\psi_{ya}}{\sigma + \varphi} \, \Delta a_{t+1}$$

Technology improvements raise the natural rate — the economy wants to expand, requiring a higher real rate to keep inflation in check. This is why the "neutral rate" fell during the productivity slowdown of the 2000s-2010s.

Matrix Form of the Three-Equation System

Substituting the Taylor rule into the IS curve and collecting terms:

System in Matrix Form
$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1 + \phi_y/\sigma & \phi_\pi/\sigma \\ -\kappa & 1\end{pmatrix}}_{A} \begin{pmatrix}x_t \\ \pi_t\end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1/\sigma \\ 0 & \beta\end{pmatrix}}_{B} \begin{pmatrix}E_t x_{t+1} \\ E_t \pi_{t+1}\end{pmatrix} + C \, \text{shocks}_t$$

The solution to this system — finding $[x_t, \pi_t]'$ as functions of current and expected shocks — is exactly the Blanchard-Kahn problem covered in Module 7. For AR(1) shocks with persistence $\rho$, the method of undetermined coefficients gives the closed-form solution $(A - \rho B)^{-1} C$.

import numpy as np

def nk3eq_solution(beta=0.99, sigma=1.0, kappa=0.125,
                   phi_pi=1.5, phi_y=0.125, rho=0.5,
                   shock='monetary'):
    """
    Analytical solution of the 3-equation NK model.
    Returns impact coefficients [a_x, a_pi] such that:
        x_t  = a_x  * shock_t
        pi_t = a_pi * shock_t
    for an AR(1) shock with persistence rho.
    
    shock: 'monetary' (enters IS via -1/sigma)
           'demand'   (enters IS directly)
           'costpush' (enters NKPC directly)
    """
    # System: A * Y = B * E[Y+1] + C * shock
    A = np.array([[1 + phi_y/sigma, phi_pi/sigma],
                  [-kappa,          1           ]])
    B = np.array([[1,   1/sigma],
                  [0,   beta   ]])
    
    # Shock loadings
    shock_map = {
        'monetary': np.array([-1/sigma, 0]),  # enters IS (after sub. TR)
        'demand':   np.array([1.0,      0]),  # natural rate shock to IS
        'costpush': np.array([0.0,      1]),  # enters NKPC
    }
    C = shock_map[shock]
    
    # (A - rho*B) * coeff = C  →  coeff = (A - rho*B)^{-1} C
    M = A - rho * B
    coeff = np.linalg.solve(M, C)
    return coeff  # [a_x, a_pi]

Solution Methods

Learn the Blanchard-Kahn conditions, state-space representation, and perturbation methods for solving linear rational expectations models.

DSGE models are systems of stochastic difference equations with forward-looking expectations. Solving them requires finding a recursive equilibrium — a mapping from today's state variables to today's endogenous variables — that satisfies all equilibrium conditions, is consistent with rational expectations, and remains stable. The dominant approach is to find a linear state-space solution via eigenvalue decomposition.

The Generic Linear RE System

After log-linearization, virtually all DSGE models can be written as:

Generic Linear Rational Expectations System
$$A \, E_t[Y_{t+1}] = B \, Y_t + C \, \varepsilon_t$$

where $Y_t$ is the vector of endogenous variables, $\varepsilon_t$ is the vector of exogenous shocks, and $A$, $B$, $C$ are coefficient matrices derived from the model's equilibrium conditions. Equivalently, using the notation common in policy applications:

$$\Gamma_0 \, y_t = \Gamma_1 \, y_{t-1} + \Psi \, \varepsilon_t + \Pi \, \eta_t$$

where $\eta_t = y_t - E_{t-1}[y_t]$ are expectation errors (endogenous). This is the Sims (2002) canonical form, used in the gensys solver.

Blanchard-Kahn Conditions

Blanchard and Kahn (1980) established the conditions for a unique stable solution. Partition the system into predetermined variables (state variables known at $t$, like capital $k_t$, lagged shocks) and non-predetermined (jump variables, like consumption $c_t$, inflation $\pi_t$).

Blanchard-Kahn Theorem

Let $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ be the generalized eigenvalues of $(B, A)$. Let $n_1$ be the number with $|\lambda_i| > 1$ (explosive) and $n_2$ be the number of non-predetermined variables. Then:

  • $n_1 = n_2$: Unique stable solution (determinacy)
  • $n_1 < n_2$: Indeterminacy (multiple equilibria, sunspot solutions)
  • $n_1 > n_2$: Non-existence (no stable equilibrium)

In the 3-equation NK model with $\phi_\pi > 1$ (Taylor principle satisfied), there are exactly 2 non-predetermined variables $[x_t, \pi_t]$ and 2 explosive eigenvalues — BK conditions are met. When $\phi_\pi < 1$, one eigenvalue falls inside the unit circle, giving indeterminacy.

State-Space Solution

The stable solution takes the state-space form:

Policy Function (Recursive Equilibrium)
$$\begin{aligned} s_{t+1} &= F \, s_t + G \, \varepsilon_{t+1} \quad \text{(state equation)} \\ y_t &= H \, s_t \quad \text{(observation equation)} \end{aligned}$$

where $s_t$ is the state vector (exogenous shocks + lagged endogenous states), $y_t$ are the observables, and $F$, $G$, $H$ are derived from the eigenvalue decomposition of the system. The matrices $F$ and $H$ constitute the model's policy functions.

Computing the Solution via Schur Decomposition

The standard algorithm (Klein 2000, Sims 2002) proceeds via the generalized Schur (QZ) decomposition:

  1. Compute QZ decomposition: $A = Q S Z^H$, $B = Q T Z^H$
  2. Reorder so unstable eigenvalues (from $T_{ii}/S_{ii}$) appear in the top-left block
  3. Partition the reordered system into stable and unstable subspaces
  4. The stable manifold gives the policy function — a mapping from states to jump variables
  5. Use the boundary condition (transversality) to select the stable solution

Perturbation Methods

For nonlinear models or higher-order approximations, perturbation methods expand the solution in a Taylor series around the steady state, indexed by a small parameter $\xi$ (the "perturbation parameter" scaling the variance of shocks):

$$y_t = y^{(0)} + \xi \, y_t^{(1)} + \tfrac{1}{2}\xi^2 y_t^{(2)} + \cdots$$

The first-order approximation ($y_t^{(1)}$) recovers the standard log-linear solution — certainty equivalence holds and risk does not affect the decision rules. The second-order approximation ($y_t^{(2)}$) introduces a risk premium: higher variance of shocks raises precautionary saving and affects steady-state levels. Used in welfare analysis and models with ZLB or occasionally-binding constraints.

✅
First-Order: Log-Linear Solution
Certainty equivalence: variances don't matter for decision rules. Fast, analytically tractable. Used for policy analysis, estimation, IRF computation. Software: Dynare, RISE, gensys.
⚡
Second-Order: Risk Matters
Precautionary savings, risk premia, asymmetric responses. Needed for: welfare comparisons, asset pricing in DSGE, occasionally-binding constraints. Slower but captures nonlinear dynamics.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import schur, ordqz

def check_bk(A, B, n_forward, label="System"):
    """
    Check Blanchard-Kahn conditions.
    
    Args:
        A: left-hand side matrix (coefficient on E_t[Y_{t+1}])
        B: right-hand side matrix (coefficient on Y_t)
        n_forward: number of non-predetermined (forward-looking) variables
    
    Solves: A * E[Y+1] = B * Y  →  generalized eigenvalues of (B, A)
    """
    eigenvalues, _ = np.linalg.eig(np.linalg.solve(A, B))
    n_explosive = np.sum(np.abs(eigenvalues) > 1)
    
    print(f"\n── {label} ──────────────────────────")
    print(f"Eigenvalues (|λ|):  {np.abs(eigenvalues).round(4)}")
    print(f"Explosive roots:    {n_explosive}")
    print(f"Forward variables:  {n_forward}")
    
    if n_explosive == n_forward:
        print("✓  DETERMINATE: unique stable solution")
    elif n_explosive < n_forward:
        print("✗  INDETERMINATE: multiple equilibria (φ_π too low?)")
    else:
        print("✗  NON-EXISTENT: no stable equilibrium")
    
    return n_explosive == n_forward

Bayesian Estimation

Estimate DSGE model parameters using Bayesian methods: prior distributions, the Kalman filter likelihood, and Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling.

DSGE models contain deep structural parameters — $\beta$, $\sigma$, $\theta$, $\phi_\pi$, etc. — that cannot be directly observed. The Bayesian approach combines prior beliefs (from theory or microeconomic evidence) with data likelihood (computed via the Kalman filter) to produce posterior distributions over parameters. This approach, pioneered for DSGE by Smets and Wouters (2003), dominates modern empirical macro.

Bayes' Theorem Applied to DSGE

Bayes' Theorem for DSGE Parameters
$$\underbrace{p(\Theta | Y)}_{\text{posterior}} \propto \underbrace{p(Y | \Theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{p(\Theta)}_{\text{prior}}$$

where $\Theta$ is the vector of model parameters and $Y$ is the observed data. The posterior combines what we know before seeing data (prior) with what data tells us (likelihood). The posterior is rarely available in closed form, so we approximate it via MCMC.

Step 2: Kalman Filter Likelihood

Given parameters $\Theta$, the state-space form $(F, G, H)$ is fully determined. The Kalman filter recursively computes the likelihood of the observed data under the model:

Kalman Filter Recursion
$$\begin{aligned} &\textbf{Predict:}\\ s_{t|t-1} &= F \, s_{t-1|t-1} \\ P_{t|t-1} &= F \, P_{t-1|t-1} F^T + Q \\[6pt] &\textbf{Update:}\\ \nu_t &= y_t - H \, s_{t|t-1} \quad \text{(innovation)} \\ S_t &= H \, P_{t|t-1} H^T + R \\ K_t &= P_{t|t-1} H^T S_t^{-1} \quad\text{(Kalman gain)} \\ s_{t|t} &= s_{t|t-1} + K_t \nu_t \\ P_{t|t} &= (I - K_t H)\, P_{t|t-1}\,(I - K_t H)^T + K_t R K_t^T \quad \text{(Joseph form)} \end{aligned}$$

The log-likelihood is the sum of Gaussian log-likelihoods of the innovations:

$$\ln \mathcal{L}(\Theta | Y) = -\frac{T}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^T \left[\ln |S_t| + \nu_t^T S_t^{-1} \nu_t\right]$$

Step 3: MCMC Sampling (Metropolis-Hastings)

Since the posterior is not analytically tractable, we sample from it using the Random Walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm:

  1. Initialize at mode $\Theta^{(0)}$ (from numerical optimization of posterior)
  2. Propose $\Theta^* = \Theta^{(i-1)} + c \cdot \Sigma^{1/2} u$, $u \sim N(0, I)$
  3. Accept with probability $\alpha = \min\!\left(1, \frac{p(\Theta^*|Y)}{p(\Theta^{(i-1)}|Y)}\right)$
  4. If accepted: $\Theta^{(i)} = \Theta^*$; else $\Theta^{(i)} = \Theta^{(i-1)}$
  5. Repeat for $N$ iterations; discard burn-in; compute posterior moments

The scaling $c$ and proposal covariance $\Sigma$ are tuned for an acceptance rate of 20–30%.

🔬
Identification in DSGE Models
Not all parameters are separately identifiable from aggregate data. Structural identification (Canova-Sala 2009, Komunjer-Ng 2011) must be verified before estimation. Common issue: σ (IES) and φ_π are often weakly identified from standard observables. Using a richer observation equation (including bond yields, firm-level data) helps.

def build_ss(beta, sigma, kappa, phi_pi, phi_y, rho_v, rho_a):
    """Build state-space matrices for 2-shock NK model."""
    A = np.array([[1+phi_y/sigma, phi_pi/sigma], [-kappa, 1]])
    B = np.array([[1, 1/sigma], [0, beta]])

    # Policy function coefficients for each shock
    C_v = np.array([-1/sigma, 0])           # MP shock
    C_a = np.array([sigma*(1-rho_a), 0])    # simplified tech shock
    
    Mv = A - rho_v*B;  Cv_sol = np.linalg.solve(Mv, C_v)  # [ax_v, api_v]
    Ma = A - rho_a*B;  Ca_sol = np.linalg.solve(Ma, C_a)  # [ax_a, api_a]
    
    # H matrix: y_t = H @ [v_t, a_t]'
    H = np.array([[Cv_sol[0], Ca_sol[0]],   # x_t
                  [Cv_sol[1], Ca_sol[1]]])   # pi_t (quarterly)
    
    # F matrix: s_{t+1} = F @ s_t + G @ eps_{t+1}
    F = np.array([[rho_v, 0], [0, rho_a]])
    
    return F, H

def kalman_loglik(params, y_data):
    """Kalman filter log-likelihood for the NK model."""
    beta, sigma, kappa, phi_pi, phi_y, rho_v, rho_a = params
    
    # Stability / prior bounds
    if not (0.9 < beta < 0.999 and sigma > 0.1 and
            0.01 < kappa < 0.8 and phi_pi > 1.0 and
            0 <= phi_y < 1 and 0 < rho_v < 1 and 0 < rho_a < 1):
        return -np.inf

    try:
        F, H = build_ss(beta, sigma, kappa, phi_pi, phi_y, rho_v, rho_a)
    except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
        return -np.inf
    
    # Shock covariances
    Q = np.diag([0.01**2, 0.01**2])   # shock std devs
    R = np.diag([1e-6, 1e-6])          # measurement error (near zero)
    
    n = F.shape[0]
    T = len(y_data)
    
    # Initialize (unconditional mean of AR(1))
    s = np.zeros(n)
    # Solve Riccati for P0: P = F P F' + Q (unconditional)
    from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_lyapunov
    P = solve_discrete_lyapunov(F, Q)
    
    log_lik = 0.0
    for t in range(T):
        y_pred = H @ s
        inn = y_data[t] - y_pred          # innovation
        S = H @ P @ H.T + R
        
        try:
            sign, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(S)
            log_lik -= 0.5*(logdet + inn @ np.linalg.solve(S, inn))
        except:
            return -np.inf
        
        K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.solve(S, np.eye(S.shape[0]))  # Kalman gain
        # ── Update step (filtered estimates at time t) ──────────
        s_upd = s + K @ inn               # s_{t|t}  filtered state
        ImKH  = np.eye(n) - K @ H
        P_upd = ImKH @ P @ ImKH.T + K @ R @ K.T  # Joseph form (numerically stable)
        # ── Predict step (one-step-ahead for t+1) ───────────────
        s = F @ s_upd                     # s_{t+1|t}
        P = F @ P_upd @ F.T + Q           # P_{t+1|t}
    
    return log_lik

  P <- Q

    S       <- H %*% P %*% t(H) + R

    s <- F %*% s_upd                          # s_{t+1|t}
    P <- F %*% P_upd %*% t(F) + Q            # P_{t+1|t}
  }

Alternative Estimation Approaches

While Bayesian full-information methods dominate modern empirical DSGE work, researchers sometimes prefer limited-information alternatives for reasons of robustness:

🔧 GMM / Simulated Method of Moments

Proposed by Christiano & Eichenbaum (1992) for RBC models. The estimator chooses parameters to match a set of model-implied moments (e.g., autocorrelations, cross-correlations) to their empirical counterparts. Advantage: robust to misspecification of the likelihood — if the model is wrong, GMM still gives valid inference for the targeted moments. Disadvantage: efficiency loss versus full-information Bayesian; moment selection is arbitrary.

🔧 Indirect Inference

Fit an auxiliary VAR to both the actual data and model-simulated data, then choose structural parameters to minimize the distance between the two sets of VAR coefficients. Increasingly used in heterogeneous-agent models where the likelihood is computationally intractable. Can be combined with impulse-response matching (IRFM) when identifying specific structural shocks.

⚠️
Identification Caution
Not all DSGE parameters are identifiable from aggregate data alone. Canova & Sala (2009) and Komunjer & Ng (2011) provide formal identification tests. Common weak-identification cases: the IES ($\sigma$) and the Calvo parameter ($\theta$) are often jointly poorly pinned down by standard observables (output, inflation, interest rate). Using a richer observation equation — bond yields, consumption data, survey expectations — substantially helps identification.

NKPC Empirical Performance: What the Data Say

The New Keynesian Phillips Curve is theoretically elegant, but its empirical performance is contested. Three empirical episodes are particularly important for a practitioner to know:

📉 The Phillips Curve Flattening (post-1990)

Since the mid-1990s, inflation has become less responsive to the output gap in most advanced economies — consistent with a rise in $\theta$ (more price stickiness) or a fall in $\kappa$. This "flat Phillips curve" means large output gaps can persist without producing much inflation, complicating central bank communication about the inflation-output trade-off.

📉 Missing Disinflation (2008–2012)

Standard NKPC models predicted a sharp deflation following the deep recession of 2008–2009. Instead, inflation fell only modestly — the "missing disinflation" puzzle (Ball & Mazumder 2011, Mavroeidis, Plagborg-Møller & Stock 2014). Proposed explanations include anchored inflation expectations, downward wage rigidity, and import price effects — none of which appear explicitly in the baseline NK model.

📈 Surprise Inflation (2021–2023)

Conversely, inflation surged far more than standard linearized NK models predicted after the COVID-19 fiscal stimulus and supply disruptions of 2021. This episode re-opened debates about nonlinearities in the Phillips curve, the role of supply chains, and the importance of supply-side shocks that the three-equation model abstracts away from. Central banks running DSGE forecasting models substantially under-predicted the inflation surge.

💡
Takeaway for Practitioners
The NKPC remains the workhorse of structural macro because of its theoretical discipline and tractability — but treat its quantitative predictions with humility. For real-world policy use, it is always used alongside reduced-form forecasting models (BVARs, time-varying parameter models) and expert judgment, not as a standalone oracle.

9.1 Heterogeneous Agent Models

The representative-agent assumption is convenient but misses crucial distributional dynamics. Bewley-Huggett-Aiyagari models replace the representative agent with a continuum of households facing idiosyncratic income uncertainty and incomplete markets (they can only self-insure by holding non-negative assets).

Household Problem — Bewley-Aiyagari
$$V(a, z) = \max_{c, a'} \frac{c^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma} + \beta E[V(a', z') | z]$$ $$\text{s.t.} \quad c + a' = (1+r)a + wz, \quad a' \geq \underline{a}$$

where $z$ is idiosyncratic productivity (Markov chain), $a$ is asset holdings, and $\underline{a} \geq 0$ is a borrowing constraint. The distribution of agents over $(a, z)$ is endogenous and constitutes part of the state space. The general equilibrium condition sets the interest rate to clear the asset market: $\int a \, d\mu(a,z) = K$ (capital).

🔬
The Krusell-Smith Algorithm
Krusell and Smith (1998) showed that the mean of the wealth distribution almost fully captures the relevant cross-sectional state for aggregate dynamics. This "bounded rationality" approach allows a tractable solution: agents use a simple law of motion for aggregate capital as a proxy for the distribution.

9.2 Financial Frictions — The BGG Accelerator

Bernanke, Gertler, and Gilchrist (1999) introduce financial frictions through a costly state verification (CSV) problem. Entrepreneurs borrow from banks but have private information about project returns. Banks must pay a monitoring cost to verify defaults, creating a external finance premium above the risk-free rate:

External Finance Premium
$$E_t\!\left[\frac{R^k_{t+1}}{R_{t+1}}\right] = s\!\left(\frac{N_t}{Q_t K_{t+1}}\right), \quad s' < 0$$

where $N_t$ is entrepreneur net worth, $Q_t K_{t+1}$ is total assets. When net worth falls (e.g., after a bad shock), the premium rises, investment contracts, output falls, net worth falls further — the financial accelerator. This mechanism amplifies and propagates shocks through credit markets.

9.3 Zero Lower Bound (ZLB)

The nominal interest rate cannot fall below zero (approximately), creating a kink in the policy rule. With conventional Taylor rules, the ZLB creates a liquidity trap: large deflationary shocks raise real rates (since $i_t = 0$ and $\pi_t < 0$ implies $r_t = -\pi_t > 0$), deepening the recession further.

$$i_t = \max\!\left[0, \; \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_y x_t + v_t\right]$$

Solving at the ZLB requires global methods (projection methods, piecewise linear, extended path). Key results: fiscal multipliers are large at the ZLB (Christiano-Eichenbaum-Rebelo 2011), forward guidance is powerful but prone to the "forward guidance puzzle" (Del Negro et al.).

9.4 Open-Economy DSGE

In a two-country open-economy model (Galí-Monacelli 2005), domestic and foreign goods are imperfect substitutes. New channels arise:

🌍 Terms of Trade (ToT)

$S_t = P_{F,t}/P_{H,t}$ — the relative price of foreign vs. domestic goods. ToT appreciation makes domestic goods relatively expensive, shifting demand toward foreign goods (substitution effect) and hurting domestic output.

$$\pi_{H,t} = \beta E_t[\pi_{H,t+1}] + \kappa_\alpha x_t$$

where $\kappa_\alpha$ depends on openness $\alpha$ and home bias.

💱 Uncovered Interest Parity

Capital flows equalize expected returns across countries. In log-linear form:

$$i_t - i_t^* = E_t[\Delta e_{t+1}] + \psi_t$$

where $e_t$ is the log exchange rate and $\psi_t$ is an UIP premium (risk or convenience yield). Deviations from UIP are empirically large and persistent (the "UIP puzzle").

9.5 HANK Models

The Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian (HANK) framework (Kaplan, Moll, Violante 2018) integrates Bewley-style household heterogeneity into a full New Keynesian model with nominal rigidities. This is currently the frontier of DSGE research.

🔑 Key Insights from HANK vs. RANK
  • Fiscal multipliers differ: With hand-to-mouth (HtM) consumers who spend their entire income, fiscal transfers have high multipliers. In RANK (no HtM), multipliers are low due to Ricardian equivalence.
  • Monetary transmission is indirect: In RANK, monetary policy works primarily through the intertemporal Euler equation (direct channel). In HANK, the direct channel is weak (only the wealthy respond to rates); most transmission goes through indirect channels — employment income and dividend income of constrained agents.
  • Distributional effects matter for aggregate dynamics: The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) out of transfers is heterogeneous. Wealthy HtM agents (high illiquid wealth, low liquid wealth) have high MPCs despite non-zero net worth — a key empirical finding from the PSID.
  • Inequality and monetary policy: Rate cuts disproportionately benefit debtors (lower interest payments) and hurt net savers — distributional effects of monetary policy become quantitatively important.
HANK: Household Problem (Two-Asset Version)
$$V(b, a, z) = \max_{c, b', a'} u(c) + \beta E[V(b', a', z') | z]$$ $$\text{s.t.} \quad c + b' + a' = (1+r^b)b + (1+r^a)a + wz\ell - \chi(a'-a)$$

where $b$ is liquid wealth, $a$ is illiquid wealth (housing, retirement accounts), and $\chi(\cdot)$ is a portfolio adjustment cost making illiquid assets genuinely illiquid at high frequency. The distribution $\Gamma_t(b, a, z)$ co-evolves with aggregate variables.

📊
Solving HANK: Sequence-Space Jacobians
The Auclert-Bardóczy-Rognlie-Straub (2021) "sequence-space Jacobian" (SSJ) method makes HANK models as computationally tractable as RANK for first-order dynamics. The key insight: compute the Jacobian of aggregate outcomes with respect to aggregate sequences using automatic differentiation and fake-news algorithm, then solve the linearized system directly.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# AIYAGARI (1994) MODEL — STEADY STATE
# Households face idiosyncratic income risk, incomplete markets
# Aggregate capital determined in GE by asset market clearing
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

# ── Parameters ──────────────────────────────────────────────
beta   = 0.96     # Annual discount factor
sigma  = 1.5      # Risk aversion (CRRA utility)
alpha  = 0.36     # Capital share in production
delta  = 0.08     # Annual depreciation rate
a_min  = 0.0      # Borrowing constraint (natural)
a_max  = 40.0     # Upper end of asset grid
n_a    = 200      # Grid points for assets
rho_z  = 0.9      # Persistence of log-income AR(1)
sig_z  = 0.2      # Std dev of income shocks

# ── Income process: Rouwenhorst (1995) discretization ──────
# Rouwenhorst gives accurate approximations even for highly persistent
# processes (ρ ≈ 0.9), where Tauchen's method underestimates variance.
# Ref: Kopecky & Suen (2010), Review of Economic Dynamics.
n_z = 5
p   = (1 + rho_z) / 2   # Rouwenhorst transition probability

def rouwenhorst_matrix(n, p):
    """Build the n×n Rouwenhorst (1995) Markov transition matrix."""
    if n == 2:
        return np.array([[p, 1-p], [1-p, p]])
    P_prev = rouwenhorst_matrix(n - 1, p)
    P_new  = np.zeros((n, n))
    P_new[:n-1, :n-1] +=     p  * P_prev
    P_new[:n-1,  1:n] += (1-p)  * P_prev
    P_new[ 1:n, :n-1] += (1-p)  * P_prev
    P_new[ 1:n,  1:n] +=     p  * P_prev
    P_new[1:-1, :]    /= 2       # interior rows are double-counted
    return P_new

Pi = rouwenhorst_matrix(n_z, p)

# Grid: symmetric, spanning ±√(n_z−1)·σ_z in log-income space
sigma_z = sig_z / np.sqrt(1 - rho_z**2)   # unconditional std dev of log income
z_vals  = np.linspace(-np.sqrt(n_z - 1) * sigma_z,
                        np.sqrt(n_z - 1) * sigma_z, n_z)
z_vals  = np.exp(z_vals - z_vals.mean())   # income levels, mean normalized to 1

# Stationary distribution of income
from numpy.linalg import eig
eigvals, eigvecs = eig(Pi.T)
stat_dist = np.abs(eigvecs[:, np.argmax(eigvals.real)])
stat_dist /= stat_dist.sum()

# ── Asset grid ───────────────────────────────────────────────
a_grid = np.linspace(a_min, a_max, n_a)

# ── Policy function via VFI ──────────────────────────────────
def solve_household(r, w, tol=1e-6, max_iter=500):
    """
    Solve household problem given prices (r, w).
    Returns policy function c(a, z) and savings a'(a, z).
    """
    # Pre-compute consumption matrix: c(a, a', z) = (1+r)*a + w*z - a'
    # Shape: (n_a, n_z, n_a) — [current_a, income_z, future_a]
    budget = (1+r) * a_grid[:, None, None] + w * z_vals[None, :, None]
    c_mat = budget - a_grid[None, None, :]  # c = budget - a'
    
    # CRRA utility (mask infeasible)
    u_mat = np.where(c_mat > 0,
                     c_mat**(1-sigma) / (1-sigma) if sigma != 1 else np.log(c_mat),
                     -np.inf)
    
    V = np.zeros((n_a, n_z))
    for it in range(max_iter):
        EV = V @ Pi.T                           # (n_a, n_z): EV[future_a, current_z]
        Q  = u_mat + beta * EV.T[None, :, :]   # (1, n_z, n_a) broadcasts to (n_a, n_z, n_a)
        V_new = Q.max(axis=2)
        pol_idx = Q.argmax(axis=2)
        
        diff = np.max(np.abs(V_new - V))
        V = V_new
        if diff < tol:
            break
    
    a_pol = a_grid[pol_idx]   # savings policy function (n_a, n_z)
    c_pol = budget[:,:,0] - a_pol      # (1+r)*a + w*z - a'  (n_a, n_z)
    return a_pol, c_pol, V

# ── Find GE interest rate ────────────────────────────────────
def excess_asset_demand(r):
    """Asset market clearing: K_demand(r) - K_supply(r) = 0"""
    w = (1-alpha) * (alpha/(r+delta))**(alpha/(1-alpha))  # factor prices from firm FOC
    K = (alpha/(r+delta))**(1/(1-alpha))                  # capital demand from firms
    
    a_pol, _, _ = solve_household(r, w)
    
    # Compute stationary distribution over (a, z)
    # Simple simulation approach
    n_sim = 20000
    assets = np.ones(n_sim) * a_grid[n_a//5]
    income_idx = np.random.choice(n_z, n_sim, p=stat_dist)
    
    for _ in range(200):  # simulate to stationarity
        pol_for_agent = np.array([a_pol[np.searchsorted(a_grid, assets[i]), income_idx[i]]
                                   for i in range(n_sim)])
        assets = pol_for_agent
        for i in range(n_sim):
            income_idx[i] = np.random.choice(n_z, p=Pi[income_idx[i]])
    
    K_supply = assets.mean()
    return K_supply - K

# Parameters
beta  <- 0.96; sigma <- 1.5; alpha <- 0.36; delta <- 0.08
n_a   <- 100
a_grid <- seq(0, 20, length.out=n_a)
n_z   <- 3
z_vals <- c(0.5, 1.0, 1.5)   # income states
Pi    <- matrix(c(0.9, 0.1, 0.0,
                  0.1, 0.8, 0.1,
                  0.0, 0.1, 0.9), 3, 3, byrow=TRUE)

# CRRA utility
u_fn <- function(c) ifelse(c > 0, c^(1-sigma)/(1-sigma), -1e10)

# Solve household problem for given prices
solve_hh <- function(r, w, max_iter=300, tol=1e-6) {
  V <- matrix(0, n_a, n_z)
  pol <- matrix(1, n_a, n_z)  # policy index

  for (it in 1:max_iter) {
    EV <- V %*% t(Pi)   # expected continuation value
    V_new <- matrix(-Inf, n_a, n_z)

    for (iz in 1:n_z) {
      budget <- (1+r)*a_grid + w*z_vals[iz]
      for (ia in 1:n_a) {
        c_vec  <- budget[ia] - a_grid
        util   <- u_fn(c_vec)
        Q      <- util + beta * EV[, iz]
        best   <- which.max(Q)
        V_new[ia, iz] <- Q[best]
        pol[ia, iz]   <- best
      }
    }
    if (max(abs(V_new - V)) < tol) break
    V <- V_new
  }
  list(pol=pol, V=V)
}
