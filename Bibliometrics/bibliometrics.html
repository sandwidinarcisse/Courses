Introduction to Bibliometrics

🔍What is Bibliometrics?

Bibliometrics is the application of statistical and mathematical methods to books and other media within the field of library science and information science. The term was coined by Alan Pritchard in 1969 as an alternative to "statistical bibliography."

At its core, bibliometrics answers questions like: Who publishes the most? Which journals are most influential? How do ideas spread across disciplines?

📌 Key Definition

Bibliometrics = quantitative analysis of written publications (books, articles, patents) to measure scientific productivity, impact, and knowledge diffusion.

Closely Related Fields

Scientometrics Informetrics Webometrics Altmetrics Technometrics

Scientometrics focuses specifically on the growth and structure of science. Informetrics is the broader umbrella term. Webometrics applies these methods to the web. Altmetrics measures online attention (tweets, downloads, news mentions).

Samuel Bradford showed that a small core of journals produces most relevant articles in any given field, with diminishing returns as coverage broadens.
🔤

George Kingsley Zipf formalized the power-law relationship between word rank and frequency in natural language — later applied to citation patterns.
🗂️

Garfield proposed and created the Science Citation Index (SCI), the first database tracking scientific citations — revolutionizing research evaluation.
📊

Alan Pritchard formally introduced the term "bibliometrics" in his paper, giving the field a unified identity distinct from "statistical bibliography."
🏆

Jorge Hirsch introduced the h-index — a single number capturing both productivity and citation impact — now the most widely used author-level metric.
🌐
2010s–present

Rise of open access, Dimensions, Semantic Scholar, and altmetrics platforms. Machine learning applied to citation networks. The Leiden Manifesto (2015) critiques metric misuse.

🎯Scope and Applications

Research Assessment

Universities, funding agencies, and governments use bibliometrics to evaluate research output, allocate funding (e.g., UK REF, Australian ERA), and benchmark institutions.

Journal Ranking

Publishers and librarians use impact factors, SJR, and SNIP to rank journals for subscription decisions, author submission choices, and prestige ranking.

Science Policy

Policymakers map research trends, identify emerging fields, track interdisciplinary collaboration, and monitor national competitiveness in science and technology.

Knowledge Discovery

Researchers use bibliometric maps to navigate large literature, discover seminal papers, identify research gaps, and find potential collaborators.

⚠️ Important Caveat

Bibliometrics are powerful tools but should never be used in isolation. They are most valuable when combined with expert peer review. The Leiden Manifesto (2015) and DORA Declaration outline best practices for responsible use.

🏆The h-index (Hirsch Index)

Proposed by physicist Jorge Hirsch in 2005, the h-index combines productivity and citation impact into a single number. An author has h-index h if exactly h of their papers have each been cited at least h times.

h-index = largest h such that h papers each have ≥ h citations

Example Calculation

Sort papers by citations descending: [45, 32, 18, 12, 8, 5, 3, 1]
Paper 1: 45 ≥ 1 ✓ | Paper 2: 32 ≥ 2 ✓ | Paper 3: 18 ≥ 3 ✓ | Paper 4: 12 ≥ 4 ✓ | Paper 5: 8 ≥ 5 ✓ | Paper 6: 5 ≥ 6 ✗ → h = 5

Related Variants

g-index (Egghe, 2006): top g papers together received g² citations (rewards highly-cited single papers more). i10-index (Google Scholar): number of papers with ≥10 citations. m-quotient: h ÷ years since first publication.

📰Journal Impact Factor (JIF)

Created by Eugene Garfield, the Journal Impact Factor measures the average number of citations received by articles published in a journal during the previous two years. Published annually in Clarivate's Journal Citation Reports (JCR).

IFyear = Citations in year Y to articles in years Y-1 and Y-2 / Citable articles published in Y-1 and Y-2

Limitations of the Impact Factor

The IF has well-known flaws: it is skewed by a few highly-cited articles, field-dependent (biology journals have far higher IFs than math journals), manipulable through editorial practices (excessive self-citation, publishing reviews), and uses only a 2-year window that disadvantages slow-maturing fields.

CiteScore (Scopus/Elsevier)

Uses a 4-year window and includes all document types (not just "citable items"). Generally correlates with JIF but captures more citations for slowly-cited fields.

SNIP & SJR

SNIP (Source Normalized Impact per Paper) normalizes for field differences. SJR (SCImago Journal Rank) weights citations by the prestige of the citing journal — similar to PageRank.

💻Computing Metrics in Python & R

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.patches as mpatches

def compute_h_index(citations):
    s = sorted(citations, reverse=True)
    h = 0
    for i, c in enumerate(s, 1):
        if c >= i:
            h = i
        else:
            break
    return h

def compute_g_index(citations):
    s = sorted(citations, reverse=True)
    cumsum, g = 0, 0
    for i, c in enumerate(s, 1):
        cumsum += c
        if cumsum >= i * i:
            g = i
    return g

def impact_factor(cites_2yr, articles_2yr):
    return cites_2yr / articles_2yr if articles_2yr else 0

compute_h_index <- function(cits) {
  sorted <- sort(cits, decreasing = TRUE)
  h <- 0
  for (i in seq_along(sorted)) {
    if (sorted[i] >= i) h <- i else break
  }

}

compute_g_index <- function(cits) {
  sorted <- sort(cits, decreasing = TRUE)
  cumsum_cits <- cumsum(sorted)
  g <- max(which(cumsum_cits >= seq_along(sorted)^2))

Bibliometric Laws

Three fundamental empirical laws — Lotka's, Bradford's, and Zipf's — govern the statistical regularities of scientific literature. Explore each interactively with adjustable parameters.

Learning Objectives

📉Lotka's Law of Scientific Productivity

Alfred Lotka (1926) studied the frequency of author contributions in chemical abstracts and discovered an inverse square law: the number of authors making n contributions is approximately 1/n² of those making one contribution.

f(n) = C / nα    where α ≈ 2 and C ≈ 0.6079

The constant C is derived from the Riemann zeta function: C = 1/ζ(α). For α = 2, this equals 6/π² ≈ 0.6079 — the reciprocal of the well-known Basel problem sum (π²/6).

Equivalently: if 60% of authors publish exactly 1 paper, then ~15% publish 2, ~6.7% publish 3, and so on. A small elite of prolific authors accounts for a disproportionate share of total output.

📌 Rule of Thumb

Roughly 6% of authors produce 50% of a field's literature (the "elite core"). This has been verified across chemistry, physics, economics, and many other disciplines.

📚Bradford's Law of Scattering

Samuel Bradford (1934) observed that a small core of journals produces most of the relevant literature in any given subject, and as coverage expands, progressively more journals are needed for diminishing returns. In the classic verbal formulation, journals divide into three zones of approximately equal yield.

n₁ : n₂ : n₃ = 1 : k : k²    where k is the Bradford multiplier

If 5 core journals contain 1/3 of relevant papers, and k≈5, then 25 journals provide the next third, and 125 journals the final third. This helps librarians prioritize journal subscriptions.

⚠️ Empirical Caveat

Real-world Bradford distributions frequently deviate from perfect equal-thirds zones. Brookes (1969) showed that the continuous Bradford distribution is better modelled as a logarithmic curve, and empirical zone sizes often differ substantially from the 1:k:k² ideal. Always verify zone boundaries with actual data rather than assuming equal splits.

📌 Practical Application

Bradford zones are used to build core journal lists for systematic reviews. The first zone ("Bradford core") is the essential collection; beyond zone 3, cost per relevant article becomes prohibitive.

🔤Zipf's Law of Word Frequency

George Kingsley Zipf (1949) observed that in any large corpus of text, the frequency of a word is approximately inversely proportional to its rank. The most common word appears roughly twice as often as the second-most-common, three times as often as the third, and so on.

f(r) ∝ 1 / rs    where r = rank, s ≈ 1

In bibliometrics, Zipf's law applies to keyword frequencies in corpora, citation distributions, and author productivity. It reflects a general principle of "economy of effort" — a power law governing information creation.

📌 Connection to Bibliometrics

Zipf's law underlies term extraction for keyword analysis. Stop words (the, a, of) sit at the top of the Zipf curve; rare but informative terms sit in the middle ("hapax richness zone") — ideal for automatic indexing and keyword co-occurrence analysis.

💻Implementing the Three Laws in Python & R

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import linregress
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ===== LOTKA'S LAW =====
def lotka_theoretical(n_range, alpha=2.0, C=0.6079):
    return {n: C / n**alpha for n in n_range}

n_range = range(1, 100)
lotka = lotka_theoretical(n_range)
print("Lotka's Law:")
for n in [1, 2, 3, 5, 10]:
    print(f"  f({n:2d}) = {lotka[n]:.4f} ({lotka[n]*100:.2f}%)")
print(f"  Sum (1-99): {sum(lotka.values()):.4f}")

# ===== BRADFORD'S LAW =====
def bradford_zones(core_journals, k, zones=3):
    return [int(core_journals * k**z) for z in range(zones)]

zones = bradford_zones(core_journals=5, k=5, zones=3)
print(f"\nBradford's Law (core=5, k=5):")
for i, z in enumerate(zones, 1):
    print(f"  Zone {i}: {z} journals")

# ===== ZIPF'S LAW =====
np.random.seed(42)
ranks = np.arange(1, 51)
s = 1.0
frequencies = 1000 / ranks**s
noise = np.random.normal(0, 0.05 * frequencies)
observed = np.maximum(1, frequencies + noise)

log_rank = np.log(ranks)
log_freq = np.log(observed)
slope, intercept, r, p, _ = linregress(log_rank, log_freq)
print(f"\nZipf's Law fit:")
print(f"  Slope (-s) : {slope:.3f}")
print(f"  R²         : {r**2:.4f}")

# ===== VISUALISATION =====
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))

# Left: Zipf log-log
ax = axes[0]
ax.scatter(np.log(ranks), np.log(observed), s=20, color='#2563eb', alpha=0.7, label='Observed')
fit_line = slope * np.log(ranks) + intercept
ax.plot(np.log(ranks), fit_line, color='#dc2626', lw=2, label=f'Fit: slope={slope:.2f}, R²={r**2:.3f}')
ax.set_xlabel('log(Rank)', fontsize=10)

Co-citation Analysis

Co-citation (Small, 1973) occurs when two documents are cited together in a third document. High co-citation frequency indicates the two documents are intellectually related. Author co-citation analysis (White & Griffith, 1981) maps the cognitive structure of a field by clustering co-cited authors.

Co-citation(A, B) = Number of papers that cite both A and B simultaneously

Bibliographic Coupling

Bibliographic coupling (Kessler, 1963) links documents that share common references. Unlike co-citation (which looks backward to what documents are cited together), bibliographic coupling is a static relationship — it does not change after publication.

Coupling(A, B) = |References(A) ∩ References(B)|
📌 Key Difference

Co-citation reflects how documents are perceived by the scientific community (retrospective, changes over time). Bibliographic coupling reflects intellectual relationship at time of writing (prospective, static). Both complement each other in science mapping.

📋Choosing the Right Database

For Bibliometric Research / Research Evaluation

Use Web of Science or Scopus — they provide citation data, author disambiguation, and export formats (BibTeX, RIS, plain text) compatible with tools like bibliometrix, VOSviewer, and CiteSpace.

For Biomedical Systematic Reviews

Start with PubMed/MEDLINE for comprehensive biomedical coverage. Supplement with Embase (Elsevier) for European literature. Use MeSH terms for controlled vocabulary searches.

For Open / Free Research

OpenAlex provides a free, fully open API with massive coverage. Unpaywall and CORE provide open-access full texts. Semantic Scholar offers influential citations and citation intent classification.

For Social Sciences & Humanities

WoS includes SSCI and AHCI — but coverage is limited. Scopus is often better for social sciences. Consider specialist databases: PsycINFO, ERIC, Sociological Abstracts.

Key Export Formats for Bibliometric Analysis

BibTeX (.bib) RIS (.ris) Plain Text (.txt — WoS) CSV / Excel Endnote XML

The bibliometrix R package and pyBibX Python package support most formats. When downloading from WoS, choose "Plain Text" with full record and cited references. From Scopus, choose "CSV" with all fields.

Mapping Science

Science mapping creates visual representations of scientific knowledge — revealing research clusters, emerging topics, and the intellectual structure of fields using VOSviewer-style visualizations.

Learning Objectives — Prerequisites: Modules 1–4

  • Interpret the four quadrants of a thematic map (motor, niche, emerging/declining, basic topics)
  • Distinguish keyword co-occurrence, author co-citation, and bibliographic coupling maps
  • Explain how VOSviewer's association strength normalisation affects map layout
  • Apply science mapping outputs to identify research trends in a chosen field

🗺️Types of Science Maps

Keyword Co-occurrence Maps

Nodes = keywords; edges = co-occurrence in same paper. Clusters reveal research themes. Node size = frequency; edge thickness = co-occurrence strength. Used to track emerging topics over time.

Author Co-citation Maps

Clusters of frequently co-cited authors reveal intellectual communities or "invisible colleges." Pioneered by White & Griffith (1981) to map the structure of information science.

Journal Co-citation Maps

Maps interdisciplinary connections between fields. Journals that are frequently cited together in papers are placed close together. Reveals discipline boundaries and bridges.

Thematic Evolution Maps

Track how research themes emerge, grow, decline, or merge over time using temporal slices. Motor themes (high centrality + density) vs. niche themes vs. emerging themes.

📌 Key Tools

VOSviewer (free, Java) — industry standard for keyword/author/journal maps. CiteSpace — temporal mapping, burst detection. bibliometrix (R) — thematic maps, factorial analysis. Gephi — general-purpose network visualization.

📊Thematic Map (Strategic Diagram)

A thematic map (Callon et al., 1991) plots research themes in a 2D space: centrality (x-axis = external links) vs density (y-axis = internal cohesion). The four quadrants reveal the strategic position of each theme.

🚀 Motor Themes (top-right)

High density + high centrality. Well-developed, important for the field. Core research areas.

🌱 Emerging Themes (top-left)

High density + low centrality. Well-developed but peripheral. Niche or specialized topics.

📡 Basic Themes (bottom-right)

Low density + high centrality. Transversal and general themes. Cross-cutting foundations.

⚠️ Declining/Marginal (bottom-left)

Low density + low centrality. Either emerging or declining. Needs attention to classify.

for p in papers:

    for k1, k2 in combinations(kws, 2):
        i, j = top_kw.index(k1), top_kw.index(k2)
        cooc[i, j] += 1
        cooc[j, i] += 1
np.fill_diagonal(cooc, [kw_freq[k] for k in top_kw])

print("Top keywords by frequency:")
for k, v in kw_freq.most_common(8):
    print(f"  {k:<22} {v}")

# Co-occurrence counts (off-diagonal top pairs)

for p in papers:
    for k1, k2 in combinations(sorted(p['keywords']), 2):
        pairs[(k1, k2)] += 1
print("\nTop co-occurring pairs:")

    for j in range(len(top_kw)):

plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax, label='Co-occurrence count', shrink=0.8)

🌍Major National Evaluation Systems

SystemCountryFrequencyMetric RoleKey Features
REF (Research Excellence Framework) 🇬🇧 UK ~6 years Informing peer review Outputs, impact case studies, environment. Expert panels. Bibliometrics inform but don't decide STEM panels.

📊Key Ranking Systems

CWTS Leiden Ranking

Focuses on citation impact and collaboration. Uses field-normalized indicators (PP top 10%, MNCS). Provides confidence intervals — explicitly warns against single-number rankings. Available at leidenranking.com.

QS World University Rankings

40% academic reputation survey, 20% employer reputation, 20% citations per faculty (Scopus), 10% international faculty/student ratio. Heavily survey-based — criticized for self-reinforcing prestige bias.

Times Higher Education (THE)

30% teaching reputation, 30% research (reputation + productivity), 27.5% citations (field-normalized), 7.5% industry income, 5% internationalization. Uses Elsevier/Scopus data.

Shanghai Ranking (ARWU)

Heavy weighting on Nobel prizes/Fields Medals (30%), highly cited researchers (20%), Nature/Science papers (20%), publications in SCIE/SSCI (20%). Favors science-heavy research universities.

Field Normalization

Raw citation counts are unfair across fields — life sciences publish and cite far more than mathematics. Field-normalized indicators divide an item's citation count by the average for papers of the same type, field, and year.

MNCS = citations / expected citations for field-year-type   (>1 = above world average)

The Leiden Ranking and InCites (WoS) implement field normalization. The PP(top 10%) indicator measures what proportion of a researcher's or institution's papers are in the top 10% most-cited worldwide in their field.

📋Responsible Metrics Frameworks

📜
2012
San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA)
Urges funders and institutions to eliminate journal-based metrics (especially IF) from hiring and funding decisions. Now signed by 20,000+ organizations. Core message: evaluate the paper, not the journal.
📊
2015
The Leiden Manifesto
Ten principles for responsible metric use (Hicks, Wouters, Waltman, de Rijcke, Rafols). Published in Nature. Key: metrics should support, not supplant, expert judgment.
🌍
2016
Metric Tide Report (UK)
Independent review by HEFCE on use of metrics in UK research assessment. Introduced the FAST principles: Fit for purpose, Appropriateness, Sensitivity, Transparency.
🔄
2022
Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment (CoARA)
European initiative committed to reforming research assessment. Emphasizes qualitative assessment, recognition of diverse research outputs, and moving beyond publication counts and impact factors.

⚠️ Module 8

Limitations & Critiques of Bibliometrics

A critical examination of the pitfalls, biases, and gaming strategies that undermine bibliometric indicators — and how responsible researchers and institutions can navigate these challenges.

⏱ ~25 min 📚 4 sections 🎯 Advanced

Learning Objectives

  • Identify at least five structural limitations of citation-based metrics
  • Recognise common gaming strategies: self-citation, citation cartels, and salami slicing
  • Apply Goodhart's Law to explain perverse incentives in metric-driven evaluation
  • Formulate a balanced argument for when bibliometrics should and should not inform decisions

⚠️Core Limitations of Citation-Based Metrics

1. Field Dependency

Citation rates vary enormously across disciplines. A biology paper may accumulate 500+ citations; an excellent mathematics paper may have 20. Raw citation counts and h-indices are not comparable across fields.

Example

An h-index of 30 is unremarkable in molecular biology but exceptional in pure mathematics or philosophy.

2. Document Type Bias

Review articles accumulate citations far faster than original research articles. Single-authored papers are disadvantaged vs. large consortium papers. Editorials and letters are rarely cited despite high visibility.

3. Language & Geographic Bias

English-language journals dominate international databases. Research in Spanish, Chinese, Portuguese, Arabic, or French is systematically undercounted. WoS historically covered fewer Asian and Latin American journals.

4. Time Dependency

Early career researchers have lower h-indices simply due to fewer years of publication. The 2-year IF window penalizes fields with long citation half-lives (e.g., mathematics, history, geology).

5. Self-citation & Citation Cartels

Excessive self-citation (author citing own papers) inflates h-indices. Citation cartels — groups of journals that excessively cite each other — have been detected and removed from JCR.

Example

Clarivate suppresses dozens of journals annually from JCR due to citation stacking — journals inflating each other's IF through coordinated citation practices. See Clarivate's annually published JCR Suppressed Journal List for verified figures.

6. The Matthew Effect

Named after the biblical passage, Robert Merton's Matthew Effect (1968) describes how already-famous researchers and journals accumulate more citations, while equally good work from lesser-known authors is overlooked — "rich get richer."

🎮Gaming Bibliometric Metrics

Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." As metrics become high-stakes, strategic behavior emerges to optimize them rather than improve actual research quality.

Gaming StrategyMetric GamedHow it WorksDetection
Excessive self-citation h-index, citations Authors cite their own older papers unnecessarily Self-citation ratio > 25% is a red flag
Coercive citation Journal IF Editors pressure authors to add citations to the journal Scopus/WoS pattern analysis; author reports

Simultaneous submission Speed-to-publication Submitting identical manuscripts to multiple journals simultaneously CrossCheck, iThenticate, journal ethics policies

🔬Negative Citation & Non-citation

Citations are not always endorsements. Studies estimate that 10–20% of citations are negative (disputing results) or perfunctory (ritual citations of foundational works). Citation analysis treats all citations equally regardless of purpose.

More problematically: influential work that is widely known is sometimes less cited because researchers assume it is known. Foundational textbook knowledge may rarely be cited in the literature it underpins.

📌 Citation Intent Classification

Semantic Scholar and scite.ai classify citations by intent: background, methodology, result comparison, or contrast. scite.ai distinguishes "supporting," "mentioning," and "contrasting" citations — a major advance over raw citation counts.

Open Citation Data

The Initiative for Open Citations (I4OC) has convinced most major publishers to release reference lists openly. OpenCitations provides a free, open dataset of over 2 billion citation links — enabling independent bibliometric research without Clarivate or Elsevier subscriptions.

✅Responsible Use: Best Practices

DO ✓

Use multiple metrics, not a single number. Normalize for field, time, and document type. Present confidence intervals. Use metrics to support — not replace — expert judgment. Consider a narrative alongside numbers.

DO ✓

Use open, reproducible tools (bibliometrix, OpenAlex). Disclose which database was used. Report coverage limitations. Consider altmetrics for interdisciplinary and applied research.

DON'T ✗

Don't use journal IF to evaluate individual papers or researchers. Don't compare h-indices across disciplines. Don't use citation counts as the sole criterion for hiring/promotion/funding.

DON'T ✗

Don't ignore publication bias (positive results over-represented). Don't treat all citations equally (negative/perfunctory). Don't dismiss non-English, non-journal, or non-peer-reviewed contributions without justification.

def h_index(cits):
    s = sorted(cits, reverse=True)
    h = 0
    for i, c in enumerate(s, 1):
        if c >= i: h = i
        else: break
    return h
